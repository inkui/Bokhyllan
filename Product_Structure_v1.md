# Product Structure v1
## The Complete Structural Blueprint for a Timeless Digital Bookstore

*Built on the foundation of Bookshop Feeling System v1 and Bookshop UX Flow System v1. Authoritative. Intentional. Highly focused.*

---

## Part One: Product Overview Structure

### What This Product Fundamentally Is

A recommendation service for books. Not a marketplace. Not a social platform. Not a reading app. Not a productivity tool.

A service that answers one question: **"What book is right for this moment?"**

The product exists to reduce emotional uncertainty and increase confidence in book selection.

It does this through:
- Deep understanding of the person receiving the book (gift-giving) or the self (self-recommendation)
- Thoughtful curation of recommendations based on that understanding
- Clear, reasoned explanations for why each book fits
- Respect for taste, autonomy, and the emotional weight of reading

### What This Product Fundamentally Is Not

**Not a book marketplace.** We don't sell books. We recommend them. Purchase happens elsewhere, invisibly.

**Not a Goodreads clone.** No user reviews, no ratings systems, no community features, no social graphs.

**Not a reading app.** No tracking what you've read, no reading progress, no streaks or achievements.

**Not a productivity tool.** No "books per year," no statistics, no metrics.

**Not a content creation platform.** Users don't create content. They consume thoughtful curation.

**Not an AI chatbot interface.** The AI is invisible. The interaction feels human.

**Not a social network.** No following, no sharing, no feeds, no community.

**Not a discovery engine.** The experience is curated, not algorithmic.

**Not a subscription service.** No paywall. No content gating. Free for all core functionality.

### Core User Journeys (Structural Foundation)

**Journey 1: The Gift-Giver**
Entry → Understanding the recipient → Building confidence → Receiving recommendations → Selecting a book → Purchasing/saving

**Journey 2: The Self-Recommender**
Entry → Articulating mood/need → Receiving recommendations → Selecting a book → Purchasing/saving

**Journey 3: The Returning User (Quick Path)**
Entry → Quick selection → Recommendations → Selecting a book → Purchasing/saving

**Journey 4: The Curious Explorer (Optional)**
Entry → Deep literary exploration → Thematic journey → Multiple books → Saving a reading path

Each journey has a different structure but the same emotional tone.

### Structural Product Philosophy

**Principle 1: Everything Serves Recommendation**

Every structural element—every screen, every route, every interaction—exists to support better recommendation.

If a structural element doesn't directly support recommendation, it doesn't belong.

**Principle 2: Restraint in Structure**

The structure is intentionally simple. Navigation has fewer options, not more. Routes are fewer, not more. Screens are focused, not multi-purpose.

Complexity is the enemy of calm. Simplicity is the enemy of mediocrity, but the product prioritizes calm.

**Principle 3: The Recommendation is Sacred**

The structural hierarchy places the recommendation at the center. Everything else is scaffolding.

The screen real estate, the navigation focus, the interaction design—all subordinate to the recommendation's quality and presentation.

**Principle 4: Privacy-First Structure**

The structure doesn't collect, track, or surveil. Personal data exists only in service of better recommendations within a session.

After the session ends, nothing persists unless the user explicitly chooses to save something.

**Principle 5: Emotional Continuity Across Screens**

The structure ensures consistent tone, pacing, and warmth across every screen. No jarring transitions. No mode shifts that feel discontinuous.

## Part Two: Product Boundary Definition

### What Explicitly Exists (MVP + Long-Term)

**Core Functionality:**
- Recommendation discovery (main product)
- Multi-step conversation flows (gift-giving, self-recommendation, explorations)
- Recommendation presentation and explanation
- Save recommendations to personal list
- External purchase integration (affiliate links, discreet)
- Optional user account (never required)
- Minimal settings (account, optional preferences)

**Content:**
- Curated book database (hand-selected, not scraped)
- Thoughtful recommendation explanations
- Book metadata (title, author, cover, basic details)
- Thematic and literary context (for deep exploration flows)

**Supporting Pages:**
- Homepage
- About/Philosophy (brief explanation of approach)
- FAQ/Help (warm, brief)
- Account management (minimal, if user creates account)

### What Explicitly Does Not Exist (MVP + Long-Term)

**User-Generated Content:**
- User reviews
- User ratings or star systems
- User reading lists or public recommendations
- User comments or discussions

**Social Features:**
- Following/followers
- Friend recommendations
- Social sharing
- Activity feeds
- Notifications or alerts
- Messaging between users

**Metrics and Tracking:**
- "Books you've read" tracking
- Reading progress indicators
- Statistics dashboards
- "Streaks" or reading goals
- Personalization metrics visible to user
- Recommended for you feeds

**Marketplace Features:**
- Direct book sales
- Subscription boxes
- Author merchandise
- Publishing/content creation tools
- Affiliate tracking visible to users

**Gamification:**
- Points or badges
- Leaderboards
- Achievements
- Unlockable content
- Rewards systems

**AI/Technical Visibility:**
- "Powered by AI" language
- Algorithm explanations
- Confidence scores
- "Because you liked X" mechanics
- Transparency about data collection

**Productivity/Reading Tracking:**
- Reading apps integration
- Shelf management (like Goodreads)
- Progress tracking
- Reading reminders
- Challenge participation

### What Might Exist Later (Post-MVP, with Caution)

**Only if it serves recommendation and trust:**

1. **Literary Context Layers** — Deeper exploration of themes, author context, historical background. But only if requested by users and framed as discovery, not education.

2. **Reading Paths** — Collections of books organized thematically, not by popularity. "If you want to explore [theme], start here" → book 1 → book 2 → book 3.

3. **Book Club Features** (experimental) — NOT social. Not community-driven. Small, private, thoughtful discussions about specific books. More like a literary seminar than Reddit.

4. **Author Interviews or Essays** (experimental) — Thoughtful, long-form context about books and authors. Not marketing. Not reviews. Literary essays.

5. **Recommendation Templates** (experimental) — "I want to recommend a book about [specific situation/emotion]." Pre-built flows for common recommendation scenarios.

6. **Gift Sharing** (experimental) — Share a recommendation with someone by link. Not social sharing. One-to-one, specific sharing.

7. **Seasonal or Thematic Collections** — "Books for moments of change," "Books about belonging," curated seasonally. No algorithm. Thoughtful human curation.

**These can ONLY be added if:**
- They serve book discovery and recommendation
- They maintain emotional calmness and trust
- They don't introduce surveillance or metrics
- They don't feel like engagement optimization
- They don't fragment the user's attention
- Users explicitly ask for them and we're confident they won't damage the core experience

### What Should NEVER Exist

Absolute prohibitions. These would fundamentally damage the product:

1. **User reviews or rating systems** — Destroy curation. Make recommendations feel ranked rather than hand-selected.

2. **Social features** — Turn it into a platform, not a bookstore. Introduce performance anxiety and comparison.

3. **Recommendation algorithms visible to users** — "Based on your reading history," "You might also like," "Users like you also enjoyed" — all destroy the human, curated feeling.

4. **Metrics visible to users** — Ratings, percentages, compatibility scores, popularity counts. Create the false sense that recommendations are data-driven, not thoughtfully selected.

5. **Notifications or reminders** — Disrupt calm. Create engagement pressure. Violate the quiet, respectful tone.

6. **Marketplace elements** — Price comparisons, seller information, inventory status. Creates shopping-app feeling, not bookstore feeling.

7. **Gamification** — Streaks, achievements, challenges, leaderboards, rewards. Turn reading into a productivity metric.

8. **Content creation by users** — Recommendations by users, lists, collections. Shifts from curation to crowdsourcing. Dilutes quality.

9. **Surveillance mechanisms** — Behavioral tracking, heat maps, attention metrics, recommendation data shared with third parties. Violate privacy and trust.

10. **Chat interface** — "Ask Claude," "Talk to our AI," chatbot-style interaction. Makes the AI visible. Destroys the invisible scaffolding.

11. **Personalization visible to user** — "Recommended for you," "Based on your preferences," "Tailored to your taste." Sounds transactional, not thoughtful.

12. **Account friction** — Requiring login before seeing recommendations. Signup walls. Paywalls. Reduce initial access.

13. **Advertising** — Any ads, even contextual. Fundamentally contradicts the curatorial, trustworthy tone.

14. **Publishing or self-publishing integration** — User-published books, indie author promotion, self-pub marketplace. Dilutes curation.

15. **Integration overload** — API for third parties, Goodreads sync, reading app connections, calendar integration. Creates technical feeling, not bookstore feeling.

## Part Three: Platform Philosophy

### Web-First Product

The product is **web-first, not app-first.**

Why:
- Lower friction to access (no download, no storage, no installation friction)
- Easier to update and evolve without forcing user actions
- Web browser is adequate for the experience (not computationally intensive)
- Responsive web design solves mobile and desktop without maintaining separate codebases
- Web feels more literary and timeless than app

**App consideration:** Only if/when users specifically request a native app experience, and only if it doesn't require adding features or changing philosophy.

### Mobile-First Design Philosophy

Mobile experiences are designed first, then enhanced for desktop.

**Why:**
- Mobile is primary use case (people discover books on their phones)
- Desktop enhancements can layer complexity without breaking simplicity
- Mobile constraints force clarity and focus
- Desktop should feel like an enriched version, not the default

**Mobile behavior:**
- Full-width, vertical scrolling
- Touch-friendly interaction targets
- Simplified navigation
- Fast loading
- Minimal visual complexity
- Clear, large typography

### Desktop Enhancement Philosophy

Desktop gets additional space and interaction sophistication.

**Desktop enhancements (not required, but allowed):**
- Slightly more generous spacing
- Optional sidebar or contextual information (if truly non-intrusive)
- Keyboard shortcuts (Tab, Enter, Escape)
- Hover states and subtle visual richness
- Optional multi-column layouts for recommendation exploration

**Desktop constraint:** More space doesn't mean more information. Use the space for breathing room, not for adding features.

### Cross-Device Behavior

**Continuity:**
- A user starts a recommendation on mobile, can continue on desktop
- Saved recommendations sync across devices
- Account state is consistent (if user has account)

**Not enforced:**
- Session persistence across devices isn't required
- A new device feels like a fresh start (acceptable and even good)
- No "where were you" notifications or "continue here" UI

**Philosophy:**
Each device provides a complete, independent experience. Consistency is nice but not necessary.

### Progressive Enhancement Philosophy

**Core experience works on:**
- No JavaScript (HTML/CSS only)
- Slow networks
- Old browsers
- Accessibility readers
- Keyboard-only navigation

**Enhanced experience includes:**
- Smooth transitions (optional, not required)
- Advanced interactions (optional, not required)
- Optimized image loading (optional, not required)
- Advanced animations (optional, not required)

**Application:**
Build a solid, functional experience first. Enhancements layer on top without being required.

If JavaScript fails, the product still works. If animations don't load, the product still works. If images don't load, alt-text and structure make sense.

## Part Four: Entry Point Architecture

### Entry Point 1: Homepage

**Purpose:** Welcome new users. Orient returning users. Clearly communicate value.

**Structure:**
- Warm welcome message (single sentence or short paragraph)
- Single primary CTA: "Find a book"
- Optional secondary content (about philosophy, FAQ link)
- That's it. No feature list. No sales copy.

**Emotional role:** This is the threshold. The user decides whether to trust this place.

**Example content:**
"Find the right book for someone you care about—or for yourself. Not based on data. Based on understanding."

Optional (below the fold, very subtle):
- Links to About, FAQ, Philosophy
- No clutter, no feature descriptions

### Entry Point 2: Gift Recommendation ("Someone I Care About")

**Purpose:** Enter the gift-giving flow.

**Direct access from:**
- Homepage CTA
- Navigation "Start here" or "Get a recommendation"

**Flow starts with:** "Who are we recommending for?" → Relationship context → Person description → Reading context → Intention → Pause → Recommendations

**Emotional role:** The user is articulating an act of love. The product honors this.

### Entry Point 3: Self-Recommendation ("Myself")

**Purpose:** Enter the self-recommendation flow.

**Direct access from:**
- Homepage CTA
- Navigation "Recommend for me"

**Flow starts with:** "What are you in the mood for?" → Context → Preferences → Recommendations

**Emotional role:** The user is exploring their own desires. The product enables this.

### Entry Point 4: Quick Recommendation (Returning Users)

**Purpose:** Fast path for users who know what they want.

**Access:**
- Direct link from navigation (for logged-in users)
- Homepage secondary link "Quick recommendation"

**Flow:** Single question (mood) → Recommendations → Done

**Emotional role:** Respects returning users' time. No friction.

### Entry Point 5: Emotional Discovery Flow

**Purpose:** For users uncertain about what they want.

**Access:**
- Navigation link "I'm not sure what I'm looking for"
- Optional: If user seems indecisive in normal flow, suggest this path

**Flow:** Visual mood board → Click emotional space → Recommendations tailored to that feeling

**Emotional role:** Validates uncertainty. Offers alternative to articulation.

### Entry Point 6: Deep Literary Exploration (Long-Term)

**Purpose:** For users wanting to explore themes or literary traditions more deeply.

**Access (Post-MVP):**
- Navigation link "Explore something deeply"
- OR from recommendation detail page, "Explore more on this theme"

**Flow:** Choose theme/author/tradition → Depth level → Reading path (3–5 books organized thematically)

**Emotional role:** Honors intellectual curiosity. Feels like a literary conversation.

### Entry Point 7: Saved Recommendations

**Purpose:** Return to previously saved recommendations.

**Access:**
- Navigation link "My recommendations" or "Saved books"
- Account page (if user has account)

**Display:**
- Clean list of saved recommendations
- Same card format as recommendations screen
- Option to purchase, remove, or explore related books

**Emotional role:** Your reading wishlist. A quiet reference for later.

### Entry Point Philosophy: Minimal Navigation

No more than 3–4 navigation items for entry points.

**Homepage navigation (example):**
1. Get a recommendation (primary CTA)
2. My recommendations (if user has account)
3. About/Philosophy (subtle, below fold)
4. FAQ (subtle, below fold)

That's it. No "Browse," no "Collections," no "Trending," no "Community."

```
│   └── /contact (optional)
└── help
    ├── /getting-started
    ├── /how-it-works
    └── /privacy (legal)
```

### Route Naming Philosophy

**Conversational over technical:**
- `/recommendation/new` not `/generate-recommendation`
- `/self` not `/user-recommendation`
- `/results` not `/output`
- `/saved` not `/bookmarks` or `/favorites`

**Predictable over clever:**
- `/about/philosophy` is clear
- `/account/settings` is clear
- `/recommendation/book/:id` is clear

**Avoids jargon:**
- No `/api/` endpoints visible to users
- No technical naming leaking into UX
- No machine-learning language

### Navigation Simplicity Principles

**Principle 1: Shallow Routes**
No more than 3 levels deep. Most routes are 1–2 levels.

**Principle 2: Avoid Nested Complexity**
Don't create routes that only make sense if you've been somewhere else.

Every route should be independently sensible and navigable.

**Principle 3: Back Button Works Intuitively**
The browser back button (or app back) should move users through screens in logical order.

No surprise navigation. No being stranded.

**Principle 4: No Dead Ends**
Every screen has a clear "next" or "home" or "previous" option.

Users never feel trapped.

**Principle 5: Home Always Accessible**
One click/tap to get home from anywhere. No "you're too deep" situation.

**Principle 6: Quiet Navigation**
Navigation is present but doesn't demand attention. It's functional, not decorative.

## Part Six: Screen Inventory

### Homepage Screen

**Route:** `/`

**Purpose:** Welcome and orient. Communicate value. Enable entry.

**Emotional role:** The threshold. Trust-building moment.

**Content:**
- Centered, warm welcome message
- Single large CTA button: "Find a book"
- Optional: Subtext explaining approach
- Optional: About/FAQ links (subtle, bottom)

**Primary action:** Click "Find a book"

**Secondary action:** Scroll to learn more (or navigation links)

**Information density:** Very low. Single focus.

**Interaction complexity:** Minimal. One main button.

**Visual priority:** Warmth and clarity over visual complexity.

**Mobile:** Full-width button. Large, tap-friendly.

**Desktop:** Centered, generous whitespace. Room to breathe.

---

### Recommendation Entry Screen

**Route:** `/recommendation/new`

**Purpose:** Ask the user who they're recommending for.

**Emotional role:** First real decision. Direction-setting.

**Content:**
- Question: "Who are we recommending a book for today?"
- Two cards: "Someone I care about" and "Myself"
- Optional: Context-setting text (warm, brief)

**Primary action:** Click a card

**Secondary action:** "I'm not sure" → Emotional discovery flow

**Information density:** Very low. One decision.

**Interaction complexity:** Single selection.

**Visual priority:** Two equal-weight options. Clear choice.

**Mobile:** Full-width cards. Tap-friendly.

**Desktop:** Side-by-side or stacked, with breathing room.

### Recommendation Card / Detail Screen

**Route:** `/recommendation/book/:id` or `/book/:id`

**Purpose:** Show full recommendation details. Enable decision.

**Emotional role:** Deep understanding moment. Building confidence.

**Content:**
- Book cover (prominent)
- Title and author
- Emotional descriptor
- Full explanation (why this works, what it feels like, voice, pacing)
- Practical details (length, tone, content notes)
- Action buttons (Get book, Save, Back, Browse related)

**Primary action:** "Get this book" (link to purchase)

**Secondary actions:** 
- Save for later
- Get different recommendations
- Browse related books (if available)
- Back to recommendations

**Information density:** High (but organized clearly). This screen contains detail.

**Interaction complexity:** Multiple clear options. No confusion.

**Visual priority:** Cover prominent. Explanation well-formatted. Actions clear.

**Mobile:** Full-width. Scroll through details. Buttons at bottom.

**Desktop:** Structured layout. Cover on left or top. Details beside/below. Actions prominent.

### Saved Recommendations List Screen

**Route:** `/saved/recommendations` or `/account/saved`

**Purpose:** View previously saved recommendations.

**Emotional role:** Your reading wishlist. A quiet place to return.

**Content:**
- List of saved recommendation cards (same card format as results)
- For each: Book, explanation excerpt, date saved, action buttons

**Primary action:** Click card to see full details

**Secondary actions:**
- Remove from list
- View details
- Get book

**Information density:** Medium. Multiple cards, but clear.

**Interaction complexity:** Simple. Click to expand, remove button obvious.

**Visual priority:** Books are the focus. Metadata secondary.

**Mobile:** Single column. Cards full-width.

**Desktop:** Single column (or two-column grid if many items).

### Account / Profile Screen (Minimal)

**Route:** `/account/profile` (only if user is logged in)

**Purpose:** Manage account. Minimal.

**Emotional role:** Utility screen. No emotional weight.

**Content:**
- Email address
- Option to log out
- Optional: "Save recommendations?" toggle (if user is guest)
- That's it. No extensive settings.

**Constraints:**
- Don't ask for profile information (name, preferences, reading history)
- Don't track behavior
- Don't show metrics
- Don't create feeling of surveillance

### Settings Screen (Minimal or Absent)

**Route:** `/account/settings` (optional, minimal if present)

**Purpose:** Control basic preferences. Minimal.

**Optional settings (if present, don't require):**
- Email notifications toggle (should default to OFF)
- Dark mode / Light mode toggle (should match system preference)
- Text size preference (for accessibility)
- That's it.

**Never include settings for:**
- Data collection
- Personalization level
- Recommendation filtering
- Reading history privacy (because there's no reading history)
- Browsing data (because there's no tracking)

### FAQ / Help Page

**Route:** `/help` or `/faq`

**Purpose:** Answer common questions. Build confidence.

**Emotional role:** Supportive. Anticipates concerns.

**Sample questions:**
- "How do recommendations work?" (brief, non-technical answer)
- "Do I need an account?" (No, but you can save recommendations)
- "How do you choose books?" (Thoughtfully, based on understanding)
- "Can I rate or review recommendations?" (No, we trust quality over feedback)
- "Is this free?" (Yes, affiliate links support it, invisibly)
- "How is my data used?" (Only to make recommendations better)
- "Can I contact you?" (Yes, here's how)
- "I got a recommendation I didn't love. What happened?" (Our fault, try again)

**Tone:** Warm, honest, brief.

## Part Eight: Recommendation System Structure

### Recommendation Lifecycle

**Phase 1: Creation**
- Recommendation is generated based on user input
- Assigned a unique ID
- Temporarily stored in session memory

**Phase 2: Presentation**
- Shown to user with staggered reveal
- User can see details, explore, compare

**Phase 3: Selection**
- User selects one recommendation
- Can view full detail page
- Purchase link is provided

**Phase 4: Persistence (Optional)**
- User can save recommendation to personal list
- Persists in database if user has account
- Can be accessed later via `/saved/recommendations`

**Phase 5: Action**
- User purchases externally (via affiliate link)
- Or saves for later
- Or abandons the recommendation

**Phase 6: Closure**
- Session ends
- Temporary session data is cleared
- Saved recommendations persist (if user saved them)

### Recommendation Object Structure

Each recommendation contains:

```
{
  id: unique identifier,
  title: string,
  author: string,
  coverImage: url,
  emotionalDescriptor: string ("Quietly devastating"),
  explanation: string (why this fits this person),
  voiceDescription: string (how the book feels to read),
  practicalNotes: string (length, tone, content notes),
  purchaseUrl: affiliate link,
  tags: [theme, mood, style] (internal, not shown to user),
  publicationYear: number,
  pageCount: number,
  contentWarnings: [] (if relevant, e.g., "grief," "violence"),
  relatedBooks: [] (optional, for exploration),
  relatedThemes: [] (optional, for deep exploration)
}
```

**What's NOT stored:**
- Reader reviews or ratings
- Popularity metrics
- Algorithm confidence scores
- User interaction data
- Tracking information

### Recommendation Presentation Hierarchy

**Primary (always shown):**
1. Book cover
2. Title & author
3. Emotional descriptor
4. Explanation (why this fits)

**Secondary (shown on detail page):**
5. Voice description
6. Practical notes (length, pacing)
7. Content warnings (if relevant)
8. Purchase link

**Tertiary (optional, for exploration):**
9. Related themes
10. Related books

**Never shown:**
- Ratings or scores
- Popularity metrics
- "Users who liked this also liked..."
- Review excerpts

### Recommendation States

**Viewed state:**
Recommendation has been seen by user. No special visual treatment. Same presentation.

**Saved state:**
Recommendation is saved to user's list. Marked with subtle indicator (heart icon, bookmark, etc.). Can be removed easily.

**Expired state (optional, future):**
If recommendations have expiration logic (seasonal books, timely themes), can show "This recommendation was for spring" or similar. Rare, used minimally.

### Saved Recommendation Behavior

**Storage:**
- Requires account OR optional guest list (stored locally or with anonymous ID)
- Persists across sessions
- User can view anytime

**Access:**
- `/saved/recommendations` shows all saved
- Each saved recommendation links to detail page
- Remove button is easy but requires confirmation

**Notification (not sent):**
- No "Here's your saved recommendation" email
- User accesses when they want
- No push notifications

**Expiration (not used):**
- Saved recommendations don't expire
- Can save something a year ago and still access it
- No "this has been in your list a while, did you forget?" nag

### Recommendation Refresh Behavior

**If user wants different recommendations:**

**Option 1: Start over fresh**
- Go back to home or entry screen
- Answer questions again
- Get new recommendations

**Option 2: Adjust last decision**
- From results screen, can click "Get different recommendations"
- Stays on results screen, new set appears
- Quick re-generation without full re-flow

**Refresh philosophy:**
- Refreshing is easy, not penalized
- Each refresh feels fresh (new recommendations, not cached)
- No "you're requesting too fast" limitations (trust users)

### Recommendation Expiration Philosophy

**None.** Recommendations don't expire.

A recommendation for grief is true whether you receive it today or six months from now.

A recommendation for summer can be read in winter (humans are flexible).

**Why no expiration:**
- Creates unnecessary complexity
- Suggests algorithmic thinking ("this was good for your mood today, not tomorrow")
- Isn't literary (books aren't seasonal or trend-based)

## Part Nine: User State Architecture

### Guest Users (No Account)

**Default behavior:**
- Can access all core recommendation flows
- Cannot save recommendations permanently
- Session-based recommendations appear and then disappear
- No email required
- No signup nag

**Optional offer (low pressure):**
- After receiving recommendations, gentle offer to "save this to a personal list"
- Click leads to simple signup (email only) OR continue as guest
- Emphasis: optional, not required

### Account Users (Optional)

**What accounts enable:**
- Persist saved recommendations across sessions
- Optional profile (minimal: just email)
- Return and see "your recommendations"

**What accounts don't enable:**
- Personalized recommendations (recommendations adapt based on conversation, not past history)
- Tracking what you've read
- Showing reading preferences
- Behavioral personalization

**Data stored:**
- Email
- Optional first name (not required)
- List of saved recommendations
- That's it

**Data NOT stored:**
- Reading history
- Recommendation history (beyond explicitly saved)
- Click tracking
- Behavior metrics
- Location, device, IP address
- Cookies for tracking

### Returning User Behavior

**Logged-in returning user:**
- Homepage shows "Welcome back" (minimal, warm, no tone of "you've been gone")
- Quick path available: `/recommendation/quick`
- Saved recommendations accessible
- No "here's what you saved" email or notification

**Guest returning user:**
- Same experience as first-time user (clean slate)
- Acceptable and even good (recommending is fresh each time)

### Preference Memory Philosophy

**What's remembered (within a flow):**
- The answers you gave in this flow
- Allows "change your answer" mid-flow

**What's NOT remembered:**
- Reading preferences across sessions
- Past recommendations
- Mood or behavior patterns
- Which books you clicked on
- Session duration or browsing pattern

**Why:**
- Surveillance feels bad, even if "helpful"
- Literary recommendations should be based on conversation, not pattern-matching
- Privacy is the default; opt-in is better than opt-out

### Privacy Philosophy (Structural)

**Principle 1: Minimal data collection**
Only collect data necessary for current recommendation.

**Principle 2: No third-party sharing**
Data is never sold, shared with partners, or used for marketing.

**Principle 3: No behavioral tracking**
Don't track clicks, time spent, pages viewed, scroll depth, etc.

**Principle 4: Transparent data use**
If we store data, we tell users clearly how it's used (in the privacy policy).

**Principle 5: Easy deletion**
Accounts and data can be deleted anytime. One-click deletion (after confirmation).

**Principle 6: No re-engagement mechanics**
No emails trying to get users back. No "we miss you" notifications. No reminder campaigns.

### Emotional Trust Implications (Structural)

When a product doesn't surveil:
- Users feel respected
- Recommendations feel genuine (not manipulated)
- Trust increases
- Product feels human, not algorithmic

**Structural decision: Privacy-first** supports emotional trust.

## Part Ten: Session Structure

### Recommendation Session Flow

**Session start:**
- User arrives (fresh or returning)
- Chooses entry point (gift, self, quick, explore)
- Enters flow

**Mid-session:**
- User progresses through questions
- Can go backward (no penalty)
- Can abandon (no nag)
- Can modify answers (easy to change)

**Session climax:**
- Recommendations appear
- User explores and selects
- Views details

**Session end:**
- User takes action (purchase, save, abandon)
- Session data is cleared (except if user saved recommendations)

**Duration:** 3–15 minutes, depending on path.

### Interruption Handling

**If user closes the page mid-flow:**
- Session data is lost (acceptable)
- User can return and start fresh
- No "continue where you left off" prompt
- Fresh start is clean and pleasant

**If user steps away for a while:**
- Session persists while tab is open
- If they return, they can continue
- If they close and return later, start fresh

**Why no session persistence across closures:**
- Simpler technically
- Fresh start feels clean
- No confusion about "did my answers change?"

### Return Behavior

**Returning to homepage:**
- Shows saved recommendations (if user has account)
- Offers quick new recommendation
- No "you have X unsaved recommendations" guilt

**Returning to mid-flow:**
- If page was closed, start fresh
- If tab is still open, can continue
- No complexity of resuming sessions

### Abandoned Session Philosophy

**If user abandons a flow halfway:**
- No penalty, no guilt, no nag
- No "are you sure?" modal
- No email asking them to continue
- Next time they visit, clean slate

**Why:**
- Respects autonomy
- Don't want to feel manipulated
- Fresh start is better than guilt

### Session Continuity

**Within a session (while flow is active):**
- Recommendations reflect the exact answers given in this session
- No caching of past answers
- Each session is independent

**Across sessions (for saved recommendations):**
- Saved recommendations persist
- No "based on your past recommendations" mechanics
- Each new recommendation flow is fresh

**Why:**
- Each conversation is new
- People change, moods change
- Recommendations should reflect current state, not past pattern

## Part Eleven: Purchase Flow Structure

### The Philosophy: Invisible Monetization

The product doesn't sell books. It recommends them.

Revenue comes from affiliate links (Amazon, Bookshop.org, indie bookstores if possible).

This revenue is **never visible** to the user.

### Purchase Link Behavior

**When user clicks "Get this book":**

**Option 1 (Recommended):**
- Link opens in new tab/window
- User goes to purchase site (Bookshop.org, Amazon, etc.)
- User returns to recommendation (or doesn't)
- No tracking of whether they purchased

**Option 2 (Alternative):**
- Link goes to a landing page
- Shows multiple purchase options (Bookshop.org, Amazon, local indie stores if available)
- User chooses where to buy
- Link opens purchase site in new tab

**Option 1 is cleaner.** User doesn't feel funneled. They just get a link to a trustworthy place to buy.

### Affiliate Link Handling

**Invisible:**
- User doesn't see "affiliate link"
- User doesn't see "commission earned"
- User doesn't see "powered by [affiliate program]"

**Transparent (in privacy policy):**
- Privacy policy mentions affiliate links for funding
- Brief, honest explanation

**Non-tracking:**
- Links don't track user behavior beyond the sale
- No pixel tracking or behavioral data collection
- No "users who bought this also bought..."

### Trust Preservation During Purchase

**When user clicks purchase link:**
- They know they're leaving the bookstore
- The link goes to a trusted retailer (Amazon, Bookshop.org, etc.)
- No surprise. No feeling of manipulation.

**What NOT to do:**
- Don't show "recommended by [bookstore]" on external site
- Don't track user behavior on external site
- Don't use data from external site to improve recommendations
- Don't show ads or upsells

**The purchase is separate.** The recommendation is complete.

### Return Flow After Purchase

**If user returns after purchase (they don't have to):**
- Same experience as always
- Optional: very subtle "thank you for supporting via our recommendations" message (rare, warm)
- No "rate the product" survey
- No "buy more books" upsell

**What's hidden:**

**Results screen:** Density 4/10 (multiple cards, each showing Tier 1)

**Card detail:** Density 6/10 (organized details, clear hierarchy)

**Full detail page:** Density 7/10 (all information, but organized clearly)

**Account/profile:** Density 2/10 (minimal settings)

**No screen exceeds density 7/10.** That's reserved for reference pages.

## Part Thirteen: Structural Calmness Principles

### How the Structure Avoids Overwhelm

**Principle 1: One decision per screen**

Each screen asks one clear question or presents one clear choice.

Not: "Tell us about yourself AND what you're reading AND your preferences" on one screen.
Yes: "What's your mood?" on one screen. Then "Context?" on the next.

This keeps cognitive load low.

### Cognitive Load Management

**Visual cognitive load:**
- Limited color palette (5–6 colors total)
- Consistent layout across screens
- Predictable patterns

**Decision cognitive load:**
- 2–6 options per screen (never 10+)
- Clear hierarchy of options
- Ability to skip or go back

**Information cognitive load:**
- Progressive disclosure (not all at once)
- Clear hierarchy (primary info prominent, secondary subtle)
- Short copy (no long explanations on screens)

### Route Simplicity

**No hidden routes.** Every route is navigable.

**No dead-end routes.** Every route has a clear next step or home.

**No confusing nesting.** Maximum 3 levels deep, usually 1–2.

**No surprise navigation.** Back button works as expected. Home is always accessible.

### Interaction Simplicity

**Minimal interaction types:**
- Click/tap (selecting something)
- Scroll (seeing more)
- Fill-in (very rare, mostly avoided)

**No complex gestures.** No swiping, pinching, dragging (except in specific contexts).

**No hidden affordances.** What's clickable is obvious.

### Decision Reduction

**MVP decisions to make:**
1. Who am I recommending for? (gift or self)
2. What's the context? (1–2 questions)
3. What's the intention/mood? (1 question)
4. Which recommendation? (select 1 of 3–5)

**Total:** 3–5 decisions before getting a recommendation.

This is low friction.

### Flow Clarity

**Every screen answers:**
- Why am I on this screen? (clear question)
- What should I do? (obvious next action)
- What comes next? (logical progression)

**No mystery.** No "I'm not sure what this is for."

## Part Fifteen: Future Evolution Philosophy

### How the Product Evolves Safely

**Principle 1: Only evolve to serve better recommendation**

Every new feature must make recommendations better OR serve users who want to use recommendations more deeply.

If it doesn't serve recommendation, it doesn't belong.

### Principle 2: Maintain restraint as product grows

The product becomes better by refining, not expanding.

**Good growth:**
- Better recommendation explanations
- More thoughtful books in the database
- More specialized entry points (for specific situations)
- Deeper context for literary exploration
- More honest, transparent communication

**Bad growth:**
- Social features
- Metrics and rankings
- Algorithms and personalization
- Gamification
- Notifications
- Engagement optimization

### Principle 3: Protect emotional consistency

As the product evolves, the emotional tone must remain consistent.

No jarring additions that feel corporate or algorithmic.

Every addition should feel like a natural expansion of the original vision.

### Principle 4: Structural drift prevention

**Red flags (signs of drift):**
- Suggesting recommendations based on "users like you"
- Showing popularity metrics
- Encouraging "engagement" or time-spent
- Introducing notifications or FOMO
- Adding features unrelated to books or recommendation
- Making recommendations feel less curated, more algorithmic

**If any red flags appear, pause and realign.**

### How New Features Should Be Evaluated

**Before adding a feature, ask:**

1. Does it make recommendations better? (Not: does it increase engagement?)
2. Do users explicitly want this? (Not: would they use it if we push it?)
3. Does it maintain or deepen emotional trust? (Not: does it increase stickiness?)
4. Is it consistent with the product's philosophy? (Not: is it trendy?)
5. Would the product be worse without it? (Not: would growth plateau?)
6. Can it be added without creating surveillance feeling?
7. Does it prioritize books over the platform?
8. Would it work in a bookstore? (The ultimate test.)

If you can't answer yes to all of these, don't add it.

### How Emotional Consistency Is Protected

**Structural safeguard: Regular audits**

Every 6 months, ask:

- Is this still a digital bookstore, or is it becoming a tech platform?
- Would a new user feel welcomed or overwhelmed?
- Does the AI feel invisible?
- Do recommendations feel hand-selected or algorithmic?
- Is there any sense of surveillance?
- Are we optimizing for engagement or for trust?

If the answer to any of these suggests drift, **remove what doesn't belong.**

## Part Sixteen: Structural Anti-Patterns

### Navigation Patterns to Avoid

**Don't:**
- Create multi-level deep hierarchies (too many nested routes)
- Use hidden navigation (sidebars that collapse, hamburger menus hiding core options)
- Create dead-end pages (pages with no clear next step or home)
- Make home hard to find (logo should be clickable from everywhere)
- Use breadcrumbs (feels too technical/UX-y)
- Create "modes" that change how navigation works
- Hide critical navigation behind login walls

**Instead:**
- Keep routes shallow (max 3 levels)
- Navigation is always visible or one-click accessible
- Every page has clear next steps
- Home is always easily accessible
- Let the flow itself be the breadcrumb (the questions tell you where you are)

### Feature Architectures to Avoid

**Don't:**
- Build recommendation engine that says "because you liked X"
- Create popularity metrics visible to users
- Build user review/rating systems
- Create "trending" or "most read" sections
- Build recommendation history ("here's what you've received before")
- Create personalization visible to users ("for you," "tailored")
- Build recommendation expiration ("this was good for your past self, but...")

**Instead:**
- Each recommendation is independent
- No visible ranking or comparison
- Recommendations are curated, not ranked
- Each session is fresh
- User history is private, not leveraged visibly

### Platform Behaviors to Avoid

**Don't:**
- Send unsolicited emails
- Create notifications or badges
- Use push notifications
- Track behavioral data
- Show metrics or analytics to users
- Create "continue where you left off" reminders
- Use dark patterns (countdown timers, urgency language, FOMO)

**Instead:**
- Let users access recommendations when they want
- No pressure to return
- No behavioral tracking
- No engagement optimization visible
- User is in control, not pulled in

### SaaS/Productivity Patterns to Avoid

**Don't:**
- Build dashboard showing "books read this year"
- Create reading goals or streaks
- Build "progress" indicators
- Create achievement systems
- Gamify reading
- Track habits
- Show statistics

**Instead:**
- Reading is private
- No metrics, no goals, no tracking
- Books are for pleasure, not productivity
- User experience is unconcerned with self-optimization

**Rule 1: Privacy Is Default**

No tracking unless explicitly asked and agreed to.

User data is never shared, sold, or leveraged for marketing.

**Rule 2: Transparency About Business Model**

Privacy policy clearly states: affiliate links fund the product.

No hiding. No games.

**Rule 3: No Surveillance**

No behavioral tracking, no pixels, no cookies for tracking, no location data, no device fingerprinting.

**Rule 4: User Control**

Users can delete accounts, remove saved recommendations, opt out of anything optional.

One-click deletion.

**Rule 5: Honest Communication**

No exaggeration, no hype, no promises the product doesn't keep.

Honest about limitations. Honest about what the product is.

### Emotional Consistency Rules

**Rule 1: Every Screen Maintains Tone**

No jarring shifts. Warmth is consistent.

**Rule 2: Flow Feels Natural**

One question leads obviously to the next.

Not a random quiz. A conversation.

**Rule 3: Respect Interruption**

If user leaves mid-flow, no guilt. No "come back" nag.

Next time is a fresh start.

**Rule 4: Celebrate Recommendations**

The reveal moment is given care and time.

Staggered reveal. Breathing room. Respect.

**Rule 5: Closure Is Clear**

User knows what to do next. Action is obvious.

No wondering, "now what?"

### Recommendation Primacy Rules

**Rule 1: Recommendations Are Never Ranked**

No "best match," no "#1 recommendation," no scores or percentages.

All presented recommendations are equally valid choices.

**Rule 2: Recommendations Are Never Algorithmic-Feeling**

"Based on your reading history," "You might also like," "Users like you also enjoyed"—FORBIDDEN.

Recommendations stand alone, based on this conversation.

**Rule 3: Recommendations Are Specific**

Each recommendation connects to what the user shared.

Not generic. Specific to this person, this moment.

**Rule 4: Recommendations Are Curated**

Fewer, better options. 3–5, not 20.

Each is thoughtfully selected.

**Rule 5: Recommendations Explain Why**

Every recommendation has a clear explanation of why it fits.

User understands the reasoning.

## Closing: The Complete Structural Blueprint

The structure of this product is simple because the philosophy is clear:

**Recommend thoughtful books to people who want them.**

Every structural decision serves this. Every route, every screen, every navigation choice exists to make recommendations better and build trust.

Restraint is not a limitation. It's the design principle that makes everything work.

The product becomes stronger by saying "no" than by saying "yes."

By not adding features. By maintaining simplicity. By trusting that calmness and trust matter more than engagement and growth metrics.

### The Structural Audit

When in doubt about a structural decision, ask:

1. **Does it serve recommendation?** (If no, it doesn't belong.)
2. **Does it maintain calmness?** (If no, reconsider.)
3. **Does it build trust?** (If no, find a different approach.)
4. **Can it exist in a bookstore?** (If no, it's not right for this product.)
5. **Would the product be worse without it?** (If yes, keep it. If no, remove it.)

If a structural element fails any of these tests, it belongs in the future, not in MVP.
