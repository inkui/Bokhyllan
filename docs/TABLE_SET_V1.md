# Table Set v1

**The first real Bokhyllan table set — designed for the Atlas → Table → Book architecture.**

This document is written from the perspective of Bokhyllan's lead curator. It is a bookselling document, not a technical one. It does not design routing logic, data contracts, schemas, or APIs. It defines what tables exist, what they serve, and how they should work as a human operation.

Where previous documents in this series debated architecture, this one acts. The architecture decision has been made: `Atlas → Table → Book`, curator-authoritative, Atlas as reader-moment router. This document is the table set that architecture exists to serve.

---

## Before The Table Count: Challenging The Assumptions

The review document recommended "10–15 tables" as the launch sweet spot. Before choosing within that range, the assumptions deserve a harder look.

**The case for 5 tables:** You want one curator to hold the whole set in their head without effort. Five tables is achievable. But five tables forces uncomfortable collapses. Grief that wants silence and grief that wants company are not the same moment — they route to different books, require different opening lines, and fail the reader if conflated. Burnout and loneliness feel similar from outside but the books that serve them are largely distinct. Five tables produces a recommendation that is technically responsive but feels generic in exactly the way Bokhyllan's voice system is designed to avoid. Five tables is a prototype, not a launch set.

**The case for 15 tables:** If you have 500 books and three curators, 15 tables makes sense. But at the 100-book launch corpus, 15 tables means many tables holding 4–6 books each. A table with 6 books has no room for the "trust me" outlier, no depth, and no ability to serve varying capacity levels within the same moment. You cannot maintain a table you cannot stock. Fifteen thin tables is worse than ten rich ones.

**The case for 8 tables:** Better. But gifting deserves two distinct tables (the buyer who knows the recipient versus the buyer who doesn't), and those two gift tables behave so differently that collapsing them produces a worse gift experience than any other collapse in the set. Eight tables requires collapsing that distinction.

**The recommendation: 10 tables at launch.**

Ten is the honest number. It is enough to honor the distinctions that actually matter. It is few enough for one curator to maintain with discipline. At 100 books, ten tables can be stocked to 8–12 books each with genuine overlap — enough to give Atlas real choices, enough to let capacity reorder within a table meaningfully, enough to hold one deliberate outlier per table.

Ten is also a commitment. It forces every proposed table to justify itself against the others. "Is this moment different enough from the table that already exists?" is the right question, and ten forces you to answer it honestly.

## The Ten Tables

---

### Table 1: When Something Heavy Has Happened

**Working name:** When Something Heavy Has Happened

**Human moment:** A reader in the aftermath of a significant loss — bereavement, serious illness (their own or someone else's), a relationship that ended violently, a death that came too soon. This is not sadness as a mood. This is grief as a condition. The reader arrives having been changed, and they know it.

**Reader intent:** Not to be cheered up. Not to be given perspective. To not be alone in something that cannot be explained to people who haven't felt it. To be met before being moved.

**Reader capacity profile:** Usually low to very low. Grief is cognitively and emotionally exhausting. The books here must be holdable in a depleted state — prose that does not demand sustained effort, length that does not intimidate, registers that do not perform false hope. The outlier on this table can carry more demand, but the lead book and most members should be accessible to an actively grieving reader.

**What belongs here:** Books that meet grief honestly without flinching from it and without promising resolution. Books that have been near enough to the real thing to know what it feels like. Books that acknowledge the specific strangeness of grief — the magical thinking, the guilt, the non-linear time. Memoirs of loss. Fiction that holds grief without dramatizing it. Short works that carry enormous weight. Books that can be read in fragments.

**What does not belong here:** Books about recovery. Books whose central movement is from grief to resolution. Books that treat loss as a catalyst for growth. Books about death from a safe narrative distance. Books that are primarily beautiful rather than honest. Books that require sustained attention the reader cannot give.

**Overlap with other tables:** Significant overlap with Table 2 (When You're Running on Empty) — a grieving reader is also depleted. Overlap with Table 3 (Something to Keep You Company) — grief often comes with acute loneliness. The distinction: Table 1 is specifically grief-as-named-condition. Tables 2 and 3 do not require loss to be present.

**Note on capacity reordering:** A reader who marks very low energy on this table should receive the shortest, most immediately accessible lead — not the most profound. The profound comes when they have something to give back to the reading.

---

### Table 2: When You're Running on Empty

**Working name:** When You're Running on Empty

**Human moment:** A reader who is depleted by sustained demand — overwork, caretaking, a long difficult season, the cumulative weight of too many obligations. This is not acute grief. It is chronic diminishment. The reader has not had an emergency; they have been eroded. They arrive at Bokhyllan with very little left, and they know reading should restore them, but they cannot commit to a demanding book.

**Reader intent:** Something that gives back rather than demands. Not entertainment in the passive sense — Bokhyllan readers are not looking for distraction — but a book that costs nothing to receive and leaves something behind. The depletion reader often wants to be surprised that they are still capable of being moved.

**Reader capacity profile:** Genuinely low. Very short books belong here, or books with very gentle pacing that does not punish a reader who falls asleep after three pages and starts over. The capacity filter on this table is the most restrictive in the set. The lead book should almost always be accessible to a reader with nothing.

**What belongs here:** Short books. Books that can be read in fragments without losing their meaning. Books whose value is in a sentence, a page, a chapter — not in sustained architecture. Books with warm or gentle registers. Essayistic books that invite dipping. Books that feel like a kind of company without requiring the reader to perform engagement. Very short novels. Poetry that does not demand annotation.

**What does not belong here:** Long novels, however warm they are. Books with complex narrative architecture that punishes interrupted reading. Books that ask for emotional labor in order to reach their warmth. Demanding classics. Books that require the reader to hold many threads.

**Overlap with other tables:** Overlaps with Table 1 (grief is also depletion) and Table 10 (Seeking Stillness, which can also serve depleted readers). The distinction: Table 10 is for readers who have the energy but want quiet — Table 2 is for readers who genuinely do not have the energy. The right book for each is often different.

---

### Table 3: Something to Keep You Company

**Working name:** Something to Keep You Company

**Human moment:** A reader who is lonely — not grieving, not depleted, but specifically experiencing the ache of insufficient human connection. This might be a solitary evening, a period of isolation, a city move, the particular loneliness of being surrounded by people who don't quite see you. The reader wants a book that functions as a presence: a voice, a person, a world that has room for them.

**Reader intent:** Company. Not comfort in the abstract — the specific warmth of a narrator or character who feels like a person worth spending time with. The reader wants to finish a reading session feeling less alone than when they started.

**Reader capacity profile:** Usually medium. The lonely reader is not necessarily depleted — they often have energy and the desire to invest it in a reading relationship. Books here can be longer and more demanding than Table 2, because the lonely reader has something to give. What they do not have is patience for cold or distancing prose.

**What belongs here:** Books with distinctive, warm, companionable narrators. Books where the voice is the first thing you feel. Episodic structures (easy to pick up and put down as moods shift) but with a consistent presence throughout. Books about genuine friendship or community. Books by authors whose personality comes through the prose. Books that feel like being in someone else's house: specific, inhabited, lived-in.

**What does not belong here:** Books with cold or distant narrators. Books where the author is not present in the voice. Books about solitude as practice or retreat (those belong on Table 10). Books that are beautifully written but feel depopulated. Formally experimental books that prioritize structure over presence.

**Overlap with other tables:** Overlaps with Tables 1 and 2 in some books (grief and depletion often co-occur with loneliness), and with Table 8 (A Gift When You're Not Sure) because broadly companionable books are often broadly giftable.

---

### Table 4: Finding Your Way Back to Books

**Working name:** Finding Your Way Back to Books

**Human moment:** A reader who has been away from reading — through exhaustion, the demands of young children, years of screen-based life, or simply the loss of the habit — and who wants to come back. They may feel slightly embarrassed that they used to read and now don't. They may have picked up three books in the last year and abandoned all of them. They are returning, not arriving for the first time, and the distinction matters: they know what reading can be, which makes failure feel more acute.

**Reader intent:** To remember that reading is worth it. To finish a book and feel the specific satisfaction of having finished a book. To be welcomed back rather than tested.

**Reader capacity profile:** Variable but the table should assume a generous worst case: very low attention span, high abandonment risk, medium to low energy. This table's books should almost all be accessible, forward-moving, and rewarding early. The first chapter must not disappoint.

**What belongs here:** Books with immediate, propulsive opening chapters. Books that are hard to put down — not because they are thrillers but because the voice is immediately enjoyable. Books with clear, confident narrative voices. Short to medium length. Books that produce the specific pleasure of forward momentum. Books that a returning reader will tell someone else about.

**What does not belong here:** Books that start slowly and reward patience (patience is what the returning reader doesn't yet have). Books that assume the reader brings significant literary patience or background. Long, demanding novels. Books that are great but daunting. Books that are primarily quiet or meditative.

**Important note:** This is the one table where commercial quality is an acceptable feature rather than a flag. A book that is warm, forward-moving, and slightly commercial but genuinely good earns its place here even if it would not belong on Table 9 (Ready to Go Deep). The returning reader needs a win more than a challenge.

**Overlap with other tables:** Overlaps with Tables 2 and 3 in some books. The distinction: Table 4 is specifically about the reader's relationship with reading, not their emotional state. A reader who is emotionally fine but hasn't read in three years belongs on Table 4, not on Tables 2 or 3.

---

### Table 5: When You Need Perspective

**Working name:** When You Need Perspective

**Human moment:** A reader who feels too close to their own situation — stuck in a pattern, living too small, or simply unable to see their life from outside. They may be in a difficult transition, or in a season that has made the world feel smaller. They are not depleted; they have the energy to read. What they need is a view from elsewhere: another century, another culture, another kind of consciousness.

**Reader intent:** To have the world get larger. Not entertainment, not comfort — enlargement. The reader wants to finish this book having understood something they did not understand before about how a life can be lived, how a society can be shaped, how a person can see.

**Reader capacity profile:** Medium to high. This is not a table for depleted readers. The perspective-seeking reader has something to give to the reading and wants the reading to give something back of equivalent weight. Demanding books belong here. So do short but dense books.

**What belongs here:** Books that shift the reader's frame of reference. Books set in different cultures, historical periods, or political conditions that cannot be dismissed as "not relevant to me." Books narrated from inside a consciousness that is genuinely different from the reader's own. Books that make the reader aware of their own assumptions. Non-fiction that reframes how the reader reads the news or understands history.

**What does not belong here:** Books that are beautifully written but do not move the reader outside themselves. Books that confirm rather than challenge. Books that offer perspective through comfort rather than through encounter with genuine otherness.

**Note on the "trust me" slot:** This table is the one where the serendipity outlier is most valuable. The perspective-seeking reader often thinks they know what kind of perspective they need. The bookseller's "trust me" here is a book that offers perspective from an unexpected direction — not the literary novel about colonial Nigeria they expected, but a Japanese novel from 1914, or a Mexican book of one hundred pages written in 1955. The surprise is the point.

**Overlap with other tables:** Overlaps with Table 6 (Restless and Curious), particularly in wonder-inducing books. The distinction: Table 5 readers want their world-view expanded; Table 6 readers want surprise. A book can serve both intents, but the lead book and opening framing are different.

---

### Table 6: Restless and Curious

**Working name:** Restless and Curious

**Human moment:** A reader who is energized and alert and wants to be genuinely surprised. They are not depleted, not grieving, not returning. They are in a good season or a restless one — full of curiosity, a little impatient with the familiar, wanting a book they would never have found on their own. They come to Bokhyllan specifically because they trust a recommendation over a browse.

**Reader intent:** Discovery. Surprise. The experience of opening a book and entering a world that could not have been predicted. This is the reader for whom "I'd never have picked this myself, and it was perfect" is the highest possible outcome.

**Reader capacity profile:** Medium to high. The restless reader has energy and attention. They can handle formally unusual books, non-linear structures, demanding prose, or unfamiliar conventions — provided the book pays off. What they cannot tolerate is the predictable.

**What belongs here:** Books that do something genuinely unusual with form, voice, or world. Books that exist in a category of one. Books that have a quality — a strangeness, a precision, a world-building completeness — that no description quite captures. Books where the first chapter is immediately unlike anything else the reader has read. Books that curators put on this table because they know the reader will not have gone looking for them.

**What does not belong here:** Safe, celebrated books that the reader probably already knows. Books that are wonderful but familiar in shape. Books that announce their register immediately and confirm rather than surprise. Books that require a lot of patience before they pay off — the restless reader needs to be caught early.

**The outlier on this table is the table:** Unlike other tables where one book is the deliberate outlier, Table 6 is almost entirely composed of outliers. The "trust me" spirit pervades the whole table. Every book here should feel like a bookseller's specific, personal recommendation.

**Overlap with other tables:** Overlaps with Table 5 (perspective) — some books serve both restlessness and perspective-need. The distinction: the restless reader wants surprise more than enlargement; they want to encounter something strange rather than something clarifying.

---

### Table 7: A Gift for Someone You Know

**Working name:** A Gift for Someone You Know

**Human moment:** A buyer who wants to give a book as a meaningful gift and knows enough about the recipient to calibrate. They may know the recipient is going through a hard time. They may know this person loves a particular kind of writing. They may want to give a book that says something specific — "I thought of you" — rather than a generic quality gift.

**Reader intent (the buyer's):** To find the right book for a specific person rather than a generally good book. The buyer's hope for the book ("Vad hoppas du att boken ska göra?") is the central signal here. They want the gift to land.

**Reader capacity profile:** Irrelevant to the buyer; Atlas must translate the buyer's description of the recipient into a capacity approximation. This is the one table where capacity is handled indirectly — through what the buyer reports about the recipient rather than through direct self-report.

**What belongs here:** Books with specific emotional use cases that can be matched to a described recipient. Books that carry a clear reason to be given ("for someone going through something hard," "for someone who needs to slow down," "for someone curious about the world"). Books with enough emotional range that the buyer's description of their hope for the book can find a match. Books that are beautiful objects as well as good books, where possible — gifts are held as well as read.

**What does not belong here:** Books that require the buyer to know the recipient's specific taste (these belong on no table — if the buyer knows the recipient reads Proust, they don't need Bokhyllan). Books that are too emotionally specific to be given without prior knowledge (a grief book for someone who isn't known to be grieving is a risky gift). Books that are primarily valuable as a reading experience and have no gift dimension.

**Relationship to other tables:** This table is essentially a bridge. Its books are drawn from other tables but selected for their giftability — the quality of being receivable by someone who didn't choose them. A book on Table 1 might also appear here if it is giftable (some grief books are; most are too raw). A book on Table 9 might appear here if it is beautiful and accessible (some literary novels are giftable; some are too demanding to give).

---

### Table 8: A Gift When You're Not Sure

**Working name:** A Gift When You're Not Sure

**Human moment:** A buyer who wants to give a book but does not know the recipient well enough to calibrate — a colleague, a distant relative, a new friend, "my book club host," someone whose reading life is mostly unknown. The buyer wants something they can give with confidence that will not misfire.

**Reader intent (the buyer's):** Low-risk, high-quality. A book that is unlikely to land badly and likely to be genuinely valued. The buyer's primary need is confidence, not specificity.

**Reader capacity profile:** Unknown, so must assume wide range. Books on this table must work for readers with low to high energy, must not require specific taste knowledge to receive, and must not have emotionally specific registers that could feel presumptuous ("this assumes you are grieving / grieving in this specific way").

**What belongs here:** Broadly welcoming books with warm, accessible registers. Books that a wide range of readers will find something in. Books that have the quality of a beautiful, thoughtful object as well as a good read. Books that are short enough to not intimidate, or long enough to feel generous — but not demanding enough to feel like homework. Books that produce the "I'm so glad I read this" response across different kinds of readers.

**What does not belong here:** Emotionally specific books (grief books, recovery books, books for very particular reading states). Books with dark or difficult registers. Books that assume literary patience or background. Very long books. Books that are primarily interesting to the literary community. Books that feel like they're testing the recipient's seriousness.

**Why this is a separate table from Table 7:** The books here are a subset of broadly giftable books, selected specifically for the uncertain buyer. Table 7 books can be emotionally calibrated — "this is right for someone going through what you describe." Table 8 books must be calibrated to uncertainty itself. The buyer of a Table 8 book should be able to hand it to someone they barely know and feel confident.

---

### Table 9: Ready to Go Deep

**Working name:** Ready to Go Deep

**Human moment:** A reader in a good season — rested, curious, with time and attention to invest. They specifically want literary seriousness. Not entertainment, not comfort, not even perspective in the simple sense — they want a book that will ask something of them and be worth it. They may have been reading lighter books for months and are ready for weight.

**Reader intent:** A genuinely demanding book that rewards the effort. The reader is explicitly seeking a book that is not easy. They want the kind of reading that takes a week and leaves a mark.

**Reader capacity profile:** High. This is the only table where high reading energy is effectively a precondition rather than a preference. A book that requires full attention every page belongs here. A book that rewards rereading, that repays the effort of difficulty, that assumes a reader who is fully present — these belong here.

**What belongs here:** Demanding literary fiction with complex structures or difficult emotional weight. Long books that justify their length. Books that have been called "important" and actually are. Books that resist summarization. Books where the prose itself is the primary event. Dense, slow, and rewarding non-fiction. Books that a reader comes back to.

**What does not belong here:** Books that are accessible and warm (they belong on Tables 1–4). Books that are good for depleted readers. Books that are primarily enjoyable. Books that are technically literary but offer themselves easily.

**Note on this table's relationship to the whole set:** Table 9 is Bokhyllan's answer to the reader who is ready for what the bookseller considers the most serious work the library holds. This table should hold Bokhyllan's most uncompromising titles — the ones the curator feels strongly about but knows they cannot give to every reader. The capacity routing to this table is the most selective in the set.

---

### Table 10: Seeking Stillness

**Working name:** Seeking Stillness

**Human moment:** A reader who is overstimulated rather than depleted. The distinction matters: this reader has energy, but the world has been too loud, too fast, too fragmented. They are not burned out from sustained effort; they have been bombarded. They want a book that is quiet in a specific, qualitative sense — not merely uneventful, but still. A book that invites them to slow down rather than commands them to.

**Reader intent:** Quiet as a quality, not as a volume reduction. This reader wants to read something that models attention, patience, and the capacity to be present with a small thing. They often do not want a plot. They do not want urgency. They want the reading itself to be a form of calming down.

**Reader capacity profile:** Paradoxically medium to high — because the stillness-seeker has energy, they just want it directed differently. This table can hold books that are not easy but that are slow and quiet in register. A book that rewards sustained attention without demanding pace is exactly right here.

**What belongs here:** Books with still, patient, meditative registers. Books where landscape or the natural world is a major presence. Books that are short but dense with attention. Books that model a way of looking — carefully, slowly, without agenda. Literary essays. Nature writing. Quiet novels about small things observed closely.

**What does not belong here:** Books with propulsive narrative. Books that feel urgent or anxious. Books with significant darkness, tension, or emotional loudness. Books about difficult subjects even if written quietly. Books that are quiet but only because nothing happens — quietness as quality is not the same as quietness as vacancy.

**Overlap with other tables:** Overlaps significantly with Table 2 (depleted reader) and Table 9 (ready for depth). The three-way distinction: Table 2 needs ease because they have nothing left. Table 9 wants depth because they have everything. Table 10 wants quiet because the world has been too much.

## Table Relationships

### Which Tables Overlap (by design)

Overlap is the mechanism that lets a bounded set of tables cover a large corpus. Every overlap listed below is intentional and healthy.

**Tables 1 and 2** share many short, gentle, grief-adjacent books (A Month in the Country, A Grief Observed, The Comfort Book). The difference is the lead book and the opening framing — Table 1 leads with books that meet grief directly; Table 2 leads with books that restore without requiring grief to be the reason.

**Tables 2 and 10** both include quiet, gentle books. The distinction: Table 2 emphasizes ease and brevity above all else; Table 10 can include longer, more demanding books provided they are quiet in register.

**Tables 3 and 4** share companionable, warm, accessible books. The distinction: Table 3 selects specifically for narrative presence and warmth of voice; Table 4 selects specifically for forward momentum and books that are hard to put down.

**Tables 5 and 6** share books that expand the world. The distinction: Table 5 leads with books that provide perspective (otherness, context, enlargement); Table 6 leads with books that provide surprise (strangeness, unexpectedness, the formally unusual).

**Tables 7 and 8** share giftable books, with Table 8 being a subset of Table 7. Table 7 can include emotionally specific books when the buyer has described a reason; Table 8 is restricted to broadly receivable books.

**Tables 9 and 5** share books that require sustained attention and provide significant reward. The distinction: Table 5 is motivated by perspective-need and can include accessible demanding books; Table 9 is for readers explicitly seeking literary seriousness and can include the most uncompromising work in the library.

### Which Tables Should Remain Distinct

**Tables 1 and 3** must remain separate. A grieving reader and a lonely reader need different opening moves. A lonely reader offered a grief book may feel pathologized; a grieving reader offered a companionship book may feel dismissed.

**Tables 4 and 9** must remain separate. These are almost opposite ends of the capacity spectrum. A returning reader who receives a table-9 recommendation will abandon it immediately; a table-9 reader given a returning-reader recommendation will feel condescended to.

**Tables 7 and 8** must remain separate. The buyer who knows the recipient should receive different books (more calibrated, more specific) than the buyer who does not. Conflating them produces Table 8 recommendations in all gift cases, which means the emotionally specific gift never gets recommended even when it's right.

## Coverage Review

### At 100 Books

Ten tables, roughly 8–12 books per table, with heavy overlap. This is the design condition.

At 100 books, every table should be stockable with at least 8 genuine members and at least 1 deliberate outlier. Several books from the Core 100 Proposal will appear on 3 or 4 tables: Gilead on Tables 1, 2, and 10; Sommarboken on Tables 10 and 7 (gift); 84 Charing Cross Road on Table 3; A Monster Calls on Tables 1 and 7.

This is comfortable at 100 books. The risk is that Table 9 (Ready to Go Deep) and Table 6 (Restless and Curious) may be under-stocked — they draw from the most demanding and the most formally unusual books in the corpus, and the Core 100 has proportionally fewer of these.

**Recommendation at 100 books:** Verify that Tables 6 and 9 each have at least 8 genuine members before launch. If they don't, consider whether these tables need to be merged temporarily or whether they can launch lighter and grow into full stocking.

### At 500 Books

Table stocking improves substantially. At 500 books, every table should hold 15–30 genuine members, allowing Atlas to make meaningful choices within a table based on capacity and, eventually, other signals.

At 500 books, two existing tables may naturally want to split:

**Table 1 (When Something Heavy Has Happened)** may want to split into "grief for a person" and "grief for a season / accumulated loss." These are genuinely different reading states, and at 500 books there may be enough distinct titles to stock both.

**Table 7 (A Gift for Someone You Know)** may want a specialized "gift for a difficult season" sub-table, because the buyer's hope changes significantly when the recipient is in a hard time.

Whether to split depends on whether the corpus can actually stock the new tables. Do not split a table you cannot stock.

### At 1000 Books

A 1000-book corpus can likely sustain 15–18 tables comfortably, with the additional 5–8 being splits and refinements of the existing 10 rather than entirely new categories.

Likely additions at 1000 books:

- **A table for the sleepless** — specifically nighttime reading, 2am company. This is distinct from Table 10 (which is about overstimulation, not insomnia) and has a specific set of books that serve it.
- **A table for the curious about Sweden** — when Bokhyllan has enough Swedish-language and Swedish-context books to serve this as a genuine reading experience rather than a filter.
- **A table for poetry** — when the corpus has enough poetry to serve a reader who arrives specifically wanting poetry.
- **A table for the reader returning from a long hard time** — recovery and restoration, which is different from both depletion (Table 2) and grief (Table 1) and becomes distinct enough to serve at corpus depth.

These are speculations. Table splits should be evidence-based (the existing table is straining; the corpus can stock the new one) rather than anticipatory.

## Gift Scenarios

The table set handles gifting through two dedicated tables (7 and 8), but the handling requires a few additional principles.

**Gift scenarios as a routing question, not a table question.** Atlas knows before it routes whether this is a self-purchase or a gift. This bifurcation happens before table selection, not within it. A self-purchase reader can access all 10 tables; a gift buyer routes to Tables 7 or 8 based on how much they know about the recipient.

**The gift buyer's intent drives selection.** The book is for someone else, but the match is to the buyer's hope for the book — "Vad hoppas du att boken ska göra för den du ger den till?" This hope is the gift flow's equivalent of intent, and it determines which of the two gift tables to route to and which books within that table to surface.

**Recipient uncertainty is a first-class state.** A buyer who doesn't know the recipient well should be routed to Table 8, and Table 8's books should be curated specifically for this uncertainty — broadly giftable, non-presumptuous, high quality, and somewhat safe without being generic. "Somewhat safe" does not mean boring. Sommarboken is safe in this sense; Mrs Dalloway is not.

**Gift books appear on other tables too.** A book on Table 1 may also appear on Table 7 (when the buyer knows the recipient is grieving). A book on Table 9 may also appear on Table 7 (when the buyer knows the recipient wants literary weight). This is correct — the same book serves different situations from different angles.

**Separate routing, not separate books.** The gift tables do not need a completely separate corpus. They are a lens on the main corpus, selecting for giftability as an additional property alongside the usual properties.

## Atlas Routing Implications

This section describes how Atlas would likely think about routing, without designing the routing logic.

**Atlas reads the moment, not the reader.** The questions Atlas asks are designed to identify which of the ten tables best matches the reader's present state. They ask: What is happening for you right now? What do you need from this reading? What can you take on? For gift buyers: Who is this for, and what do you hope it does?

**Most moments route clearly.** A reader who reports burnout routes to Table 2. A reader who reports wanting to be surprised routes to Table 6. A reader who cannot identify their state routes to the overflow table (which would be Table 3 — companionship is the safest broad default, and broadly companionable books are rarely wrong).

**Ambiguous moments are common and should be designed for.** A reader who is both depleted and lonely (Tables 2 and 3) should route to whichever table produces the better-matched lead book for their reported capacity. The overlap in the two tables means the actual book surfaced may be similar; the framing in the reveal copy will differ.

**Capacity is a within-table safeguard, not a routing signal.** Atlas uses capacity to ensure the lead book from a table is not too demanding for the reported state, not to choose between tables. A very depleted reader routed to Table 5 (perspective) should receive the lightest, most accessible perspective-offering book on that table, not be re-routed to Table 2.

**The gift routing is a separate front door.** A buyer identifies as a gift buyer early. Atlas then asks about the recipient and the buyer's hope — different questions, different signals, routes to Tables 7 or 8.

**The outlier is Atlas's serendipity tool.** Each table has a designated "trust me" book. Atlas can surface it occasionally — not randomly, but through a curated selection by the curator that is valid for this table. The outlier is not the lead unless the lead has already been recently given to this flow's context (in a session-stateless system, this is less relevant, but the outlier's placement can be varied by the curator periodically).

## Curator Workload

A single curator can realistically maintain this table set. But "maintain" needs to be broken into its actual components.

**Initial composition (one-time, front-loaded):** Building 10 well-stocked tables from 100 books is a few days' work for a curator who knows the books. The Core 100 Proposal already provides the material; the work is assignment and ordering, not selection. Estimate: 2–4 days total, concentrated at launch.

**Per-book placement (ongoing):** Each new book added to the corpus should be evaluated for table membership — which tables does this book belong on, and what does it do there? At the 10-min-per-book review pace established in the Atlas foundation, this is the main ongoing labor cost. At 50 books added per year, that's roughly a week of curator time annually on placement alone.

**Table review (quarterly):** Each table should be reviewed once per quarter — is the lead book still right? Are any members stale or misplaced? Is any table dangerously thin? This is an hour per table, 10 tables, roughly 10 hours per quarter. Manageable.

**Staleness risk:** The most important risk is a table that is composed and forgotten. Tables composed in 2024 may have a lead book that was perfect for a 50-book corpus but is no longer the right choice at 200. The quarterly review cadence exists specifically for this.

**Single-curator fragility:** A single-curator operation is fragile. If that curator leaves, the knowledge of why books are placed where they are is at risk. The mitigation is not technical — it is documentation. Each table should have a brief written rationale for every member (not enrichment copy, just placement notes: "why is this book on this table?"). This makes onboarding a second curator significantly easier.

**Verdict:** One curator, sustained. Not one curator, effortless. There is real ongoing labor, and it is the right kind of labor: reading, judgment, placement. This is not a paperwork burden; it is the curator's work expressed at the table level.

## Stress Test: 1000 Books

**What happens first?**

Table stocking richens substantially — most tables hold 40–80 books, and Atlas can be much more precise within a table. That part improves.

What breaks first is **table staleness at scale**. At 1000 books, the lead book on a table may have been chosen when the library had 100 books. The reasoning for that choice was sound in 2024. In 2027, with three years of new books and a richer corpus, that lead book may no longer be the best answer — but if the quarterly review has lapsed or the original reasoning was never written down, the curator may not notice.

**The second thing that breaks** is the outlier slot. At 1000 books, the "trust me" book on each table should be refreshed periodically to remain genuinely surprising. If the outlier becomes the same book for five years, it is no longer an outlier — it is a poorly-positioned lead. Refreshing outliers requires the curator to genuinely track what has been surfaced and when, which is labor that may not have a home in the review cadence.

**The third thing that breaks** is the assumption that tables are still the right granularity. At 1000 books, the corpus may have enough grief-specifically-around-death-of-a-parent books to constitute a meaningful sub-table. Whether this sub-table should exist depends on whether the moment it serves is genuinely distinct and whether the curator can stock it. The risk is that tables proliferate past the point of maintainability, which the review document warns against at 50 tables.

**The healthy failure mode:** Tables that are slightly over-stocked and slightly under-refreshed — this is a quality degradation, not a collapse. The table system does not fail catastrophically; it becomes gradually less precisely curated. This is a recoverable problem. A curator can re-read a table and rebalance it.

**The unhealthy failure mode:** A lead book that is no longer the right choice on its table, surfaced thousands of times before anyone notices. Mitigation: the quarterly review must include explicit evaluation of the lead book against the current corpus, not just against the table's current membership.

**What does not break at 1000 books:** The architecture. Ten tables over a 1000-book corpus work better than ten tables over a 100-book corpus — more books means more overlap, more choices within a table, better capacity calibration. The table model's scalability is real; the risk is curatorial maintenance, not structural failure.

## Final Recommendation

**Bokhyllan should launch with exactly these ten tables, in this order of build priority:**

| Build priority | Table | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | When Something Heavy Has Happened | Most emotionally consequential; must be right |
| 2 | When You're Running on Empty | Most common modern reading state; must be well-stocked |
| 3 | A Gift When You're Not Sure | Handles uncertainty gracefully; highest volume gift use case |
| 4 | Finding Your Way Back to Books | Welcoming new and returning readers; important for product trust |
| 5 | Something to Keep You Company | Broadest overlap with other tables; defensive value |
| 6 | A Gift for Someone You Know | More calibrated gift experience; requires more curatorial work |
| 7 | Seeking Stillness | Bokhyllan's most distinctive register |
| 8 | When You Need Perspective | Serves a specific intent well once stocked |
| 9 | Ready to Go Deep | Highest ceiling; can afford to launch slightly under-stocked |
| 10 | Restless and Curious | Most dependent on unusual books; needs a rich corpus |

**The ten tables are launch-ready when:**
- Each has at least 8 genuine members from the approved corpus
- Each has a designated lead book and at least one designated outlier
- Each has a written sentence explaining why the lead book is the lead
- None of the tables has more than 40% overlap with any other single table (overlap is healthy; dominance is not)
- The gift tables (7 and 8) have been verified to not require knowing the recipient's reading life

**The ten tables are not ready when:**
- Any table has fewer than 6 genuine members
- Any table's lead book does not clearly serve the described human moment
- Tables 7 and 8 draw from exactly the same book set (they should share books but not be identical)
- Tables 1, 2, and 10 cannot be meaningfully distinguished to a reader by their opening framing

**What these tables do not try to do:** Cover every possible reader. A reader who wants a thriller, a reader who wants genre fantasy by the series, a reader who wants popular celebrity memoir — none of these readers is well-served by this table set, and the tables are not pretending otherwise. The tables serve Bokhyllan's actual reader: someone who wants a literary bookseller's recommendation for a real human moment. That reader is the reason these tables exist. Trying to serve everyone by adding more tables would make every table worse.

**The success metric for the table set:** A curator, looking at a table, can say: "Yes, I would recommend this book to a reader in this moment. I know why it's there. If it were wrong, I would know how to replace it."

That is the table system working. Not "did the score converge." Not "is the coverage complete." Whether a person, reading the table, recognizes it as a judgment worth standing behind.

*This document is the curatorial core of the Atlas v2 architecture. Tables are not derived from the corpus — they are the reason the corpus is organized the way it is. Every book added to Bokhyllan should be asked: which table does this serve? If the answer is none, the book may still belong in the library, but the table set has a gap that should be named.*
