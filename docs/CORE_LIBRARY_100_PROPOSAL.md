# Core Library 100 — Proposal v1

**The first 100 books Bokhyllan would choose to place on its shelves.**

This is not a ranking. It is not a best-of list. It is a library — a space with a character, a set of values, and a consistent point of view. Every book here was chosen to serve a specific reader in a specific moment, and to contribute something the others do not.

The proposal is organised into twelve categories. Each category serves a distinct reading situation. Together they cover the range of human moments Bokhyllan is designed to meet — without attempting to cover everything, which would make the library indistinct.

Books marked **Core** would survive a launch with 50 titles. Books marked **Extended** are essential to a library of 100 but are not the foundation.

---

## Curation Principles

**Emotional precision over coverage.** A book earns its place by having a specific quality of reading experience — something that can be described honestly from inside the reading, that is not available from any other book in the library. A book that overlaps completely with another book already here does not earn its place, regardless of how good it is.

**Rereadability.** Bokhyllan recommends books for moments. A book that only works once, that cannot be returned to, is a weaker candidate than one that reveals different things at different readings. Most books here can be lived with, not just consumed.

**Giftability.** Not a genre — a quality. A book is giftable when it can be placed in another person's hands with quiet confidence that it will land. This requires clarity of register, warmth that does not condescend, and the absence of elements that would make it hard to receive. Not every book here is giftable, but the library should have enough that are.

**No competition between categories.** Each category is a different room. A reader entering Grief & Loss is not competing with a reader entering Wonder & Imagination. The categories do not rank against each other.

**Swedish identity is not ornamental.** The Swedish Voices section is not diversity signaling — it is foundational. A Swedish literary bookseller that recommends only international titles is not a Swedish literary bookseller. Swedish literature is part of the library's core identity.

## 1. Literary Warmth

*8 books — 3 Core, 5 Extended*

**Why this category:** Not all warmth is the same. This category is for books where warmth is the primary register but not the only quality — books that are warm and true, that have earned their warmth through honesty about difficulty and loss. These are not comfort reads in the easy sense. They are books where the world is ultimately held with love.

**Who arrives here:** Someone who wants to be held by a book — but is not yet sure they need the darker rooms.

---

**Gilead — Marilynne Robinson** · *Core*
The standard against which Bokhyllan's warmth is measured. A dying father writing to a son who will not remember him — and the prose that emerges is luminous, unhurried, and true. Not sentimental. Warm in the way that a late afternoon is warm: specific, and running out.

**Crossing to Safety — Wallace Stegner** · *Core*
Two couples, deep friendship over decades, Vermont summers. Stegner's prose is precise and affectionate and attentive to what the shape of a life actually looks like — including its tensions, its disappointments, and the particular warmth of love that has survived time. Less celebrated than it deserves.

**A Month in the Country — J.L. Carr** · *Core*
A very short novel about a man restoring a medieval church fresco in Yorkshire after the First World War. About healing through work, summer, and unexpected connection. One of the most quietly perfect books in English, and almost unknown. Core because it is exactly what Bokhyllan exists to discover.

**Housekeeping — Marilynne Robinson** · *Extended*
Robinson's first novel, and unlike Gilead in register — stranger, more haunting, more purely literary. Two sisters raised by a drifting aunt in a small Idaho town. The warmth here is strange and wild rather than still and luminous. Paired with Gilead in the library, they show how wide warmth's range can be.

**A Gentleman in Moscow — Amor Towles** · *Extended*
A count under house arrest in the Metropol Hotel for decades makes a beautiful life within severe constraints. Long, warm, witty, and immersive. The book's thesis is that a life can be made magnificent within almost any limitation — and it makes the case convincingly.

**Olive Kitteridge — Elizabeth Strout** · *Extended*
Stories set in Crosby, Maine, connected by their difficult, loving, exasperating central figure. Strout's warmth is earned — Olive is neither likeable nor comfortable, and the book does not pretend otherwise. What it shows instead is how warmth and difficulty coexist in real relationships over time.

**Commonwealth — Ann Patchett** · *Extended*
A novel about two families joined by an impulsive act and what happens across the next fifty years. Patchett writes with great warmth about the bonds families make and the ones they fail to make. Not sentimental: the book contains real loss and real disappointment alongside its warmth.

**The Snow Child — Eowyn Ivey** · *Extended*
Alaska, a childless couple, a child who appears from the snow. A retelling of a Russian fairy tale that earns its register completely. The landscape is vivid and the warmth is the winter kind — fierce, finite, and beautiful precisely because it does not last.

## 2. Quiet Lives

*8 books — 5 Core, 3 Extended*

**Why this category:** Books about ordinary lives observed with extraordinary attention. These are not books in which dramatic things happen. They are books in which the reader is asked to stay with a life — to accompany it, to find what it contains. The effect of a good quiet-life novel is that the reader's own life looks larger afterward.

**Who arrives here:** Someone who is questioning the value of their own unremarkable life. Someone who has been told by the world that nothing important has happened to them.

---

**Stoner — John Williams** · *Core*
The foundational quiet-life novel. A man becomes a university professor and lives an entirely ordinary life — failed marriage, academic disappointments, a late love affair, unremarkable death. Williams gives this life such precise attention that finishing it produces the sensation of having understood something about what a life is. The "Stoner effect" is real and documented.

**Remains of the Day — Kazuo Ishiguro** · *Core*
Stevens, an English butler, takes a rare motor trip and slowly, politely, fails to tell us what his life actually contained. The unreliable narration is Ishiguro's mechanism: the reader sees what Stevens cannot. About dignity, service, the choices made in its name, and what is lost when feeling is suppressed for too long.

**My Ántonia — Willa Cather** · *Core*
An immigrant girl grows up on the Nebraska prairie and becomes, in the memory of the narrator who loved her, something like the land itself. Cather's prose is spacious and exact, and the book is about the way a particular person can become the place they inhabit — and what that costs.

**Mrs Bridge — Evan S. Connell** · *Core*
A novel in fragments — very short chapters, each a moment in the life of an upper-middle-class Kansas City woman in the mid-twentieth century. Nothing dramatic. The effect is devastating. Connell is interested in what a careful, conventional life leaves unexpressed, and the book holds that question open with complete precision.

**Giovanni's Room — James Baldwin** · *Core*
A young American in Paris and the love he cannot allow himself to have. Baldwin's prose is extraordinary — clear, aching, precise. The novel is about the quiet violence of self-suppression and what it costs. Short, essential, and one of the most precisely observed novels in American literature.

**The Hours — Michael Cunningham** · *Extended*
Three women, three days, three versions of the same question: what kind of life is possible? Cunningham's novel exists in relationship to Virginia Woolf's Mrs Dalloway and earns that relationship. About the quiet heroism of living with difficulty.

**A Tree Grows in Brooklyn — Betty Smith** · *Extended*
A girl grows up in early-twentieth-century Williamsburg, Brooklyn, in poverty, surrounded by books. Betty Smith's warmth is unsentimental — the book is honest about the cost of poverty — but its love for Francie Nolan and her world is absolute. One of the great coming-of-age novels, and more widely read than it should be in Swedish literary circles.

**The Enchanted April — Elizabeth von Arnim** · *Extended*
Four very different women rent an Italian castle for a month. Nothing much happens, and yet everything changes. Von Arnim's lightness is not superficial — the book is genuinely wise about what a brief, beautiful month can do to a life that has become gray. Giftable.

## 3. Wonder & Imagination

*8 books — 5 Core, 3 Extended*

**Why this category:** Books where the world is larger, stranger, and more beautiful than it seemed before the reading. Not children's wonder — the wonder of a mind encountering something it could not have predicted. These books are not escapist in the pejorative sense; they expand what is real.

**Who arrives here:** Someone who is restless and curious. Someone who has not been surprised in a long time. Someone who wants to go somewhere that does not exist.

---

**Piranesi — Susanna Clarke** · *Core*
A man lives in an infinite house of halls and tides and statues, and the reader slowly understands what this means. Clarke's world is total and strange and beautiful. The book restores attention by giving it somewhere entirely specific to go. Probably the most perfectly calibrated book in the library for its intended reader.

**Invisible Cities — Italo Calvino** · *Core*
Marco Polo describes imaginary cities to Kublai Khan. Each city is a meditation on memory, desire, trade, loss, and time. The book is short and can be read in any order — but it accumulates something. One of the great achievements of the literary imagination, and a book Bokhyllan can place with complete confidence to the right reader.

**The Little Prince — Antoine de Saint-Exupéry** · *Core*
Ostensibly for children. Not for children. A pilot stranded in the Sahara meets a little prince from another planet — and the encounter is about what grown-ups lose and what they refuse to remember. Short, essential, giftable in a way few books are. Bokhyllan needs this on its shelves.

**The Master and Margarita — Mikhail Bulgakov** · *Core*
The Devil visits Stalin's Moscow with an entourage that includes a giant talking cat. Written in secret during the Soviet era and not published until after Bulgakov's death. Furiously alive, funny, dark, and structurally magnificent. One of the great novels of the twentieth century that does not always appear on lists of great novels.

**One Hundred Years of Solitude — Gabriel García Márquez** · *Core*
The Buendía family across seven generations in the mythical town of Macondo. The foundation of magical realism. An entire world, an entire century, an entire way of understanding how myth and history become indistinguishable. Long, immersive, and essential. A Core book even though it demands sustained commitment.

**Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell — Susanna Clarke** · *Extended*
Two magicians attempt to restore magic to England during the Napoleonic War. Eight hundred pages of the best Austenesque prose written in the twenty-first century. Dark at its center, funny in its footnotes, and deeply committed to its invented world. Demands significant reading commitment — Extended because not every reader is ready for it.

**The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle — Haruki Murakami** · *Extended*
A man searches for his cat and finds something much stranger. Murakami at full immersive power — Tokyo, dreams, history, a woman in a tower, a dry well. Long and absorbing and unlike anything else. Serves a reader who is ready to give a book their complete trust.

**The Hobbit — J.R.R. Tolkien** · *Extended*
A comfort-loving hobbit is swept into an adventure he did not choose. The grandfather of all "there and back again" stories — warm, funny, narrated directly to the reader, and asking almost nothing in return. Bokhyllan includes this not as a concession to popularity but because the narrator's voice is genuine company.

## 4. Grief & Loss

*8 books — 6 Core, 2 Extended*

**Why this category:** Books that meet grief honestly, without promising resolution or comfort that is not earned. These are not books about getting over loss — they are books that hold loss with enough precision that a grieving reader feels less alone in the specific strangeness of what they are going through.

**Who arrives here:** Someone in acute grief. Someone who has not yet found language for what they are carrying. Someone who wants acknowledgement before comfort.

---

**The Year of Magical Thinking — Joan Didion** · *Core*
Grief made precise. Didion documents the year after her husband's sudden death with the analytical calm of a scientist, which makes the impact arrive sideways. Not comforting. Not therapeutic. A record of what grief actually does to a mind — the magical thinking, the bargaining, the way time stops making sense. For the reader who wants honesty before anything else.

**A Monster Calls — Patrick Ness** · *Core*
A boy is visited by a monster while his mother is dying. The monster wants the truth — and the truth, when it arrives, is the specific guilt that anticipatory grief produces and that cannot usually be spoken. Short, illustrated, devastating in the way only a book that gives permission can be. One of the few books in this library that I would hand to a child or an adult with equal confidence.

**When Breath Becomes Air — Paul Kalanithi** · *Core*
A neurosurgeon receives a terminal cancer diagnosis and writes about what he finds on the other side of that knowledge — about what makes a life worth living, what medicine can and cannot offer, and what it means to build something that will outlast you. Unfinished at his death, completed by his wife. One of the most honest books about mortality in print.

**H is for Hawk — Helen Macdonald** · *Core*
After her father's death, Macdonald trains a goshawk and writes about what the training does to grief. About wildness, control, the strange therapy of an extreme obsession, and what hawks see that humans don't. The nature writing is extraordinary; the grief writing is more extraordinary. A book that does something no other book on this list does.

**Beloved — Toni Morrison** · *Core*
A woman who escaped slavery is haunted by the ghost of the daughter she killed rather than allow to be recaptured. Morrison's prose is unlike anything else in American literature — dense, incantatory, and impossible to read without its full weight. A Core book despite its difficulty because the reading states it serves — grief for the unthinkable, for the inheritance of violence — are not served elsewhere in the library.

**The Diving Bell and the Butterfly — Jean-Dominique Bauby** · *Core*
Bauby, the editor of French Elle, suffers a stroke that leaves him with locked-in syndrome — he can move only his left eye. He dictates this memoir by blinking. Fifty-six days after it was published, he died. It is a short, fierce, beautiful book about what a body can still contain when it can no longer move.

**A Grief Observed — C.S. Lewis** · *Extended*
Lewis's notebooks after the death of his wife, published under a pseudonym. Brief, raw, and honest about the ways grief challenges faith. Less literary than the others here — it is essentially a private journal — but specific and truthful in a way that earns its place.

**Grief Is the Thing with Feathers — Max Porter** · *Extended*
A fragmented novel-in-prose-poems: a man and his two sons after the death of their mother, visited by a Crow figure from Ted Hughes. Short, strange, formally original. Not for every reader of this library — but for the reader ready for grief in this particular compressed register, nothing else does what it does.

## 5. Companionship

*8 books — 0 Core, 8 Extended*

**Why this category:** All Extended by design. Companionship books are not foundational — they are what makes a library feel generous. These are books whose primary gift is the quality of their company: the voice, the world, the feeling of being with someone specific. No drama required. Just presence.

**Who arrives here:** Someone who is lonely or isolated. Someone who wants a book that will simply be there. Someone who does not need to be challenged today.

---

**All Creatures Great and Small — James Herriot** · *Extended*
A country vet in the Yorkshire Dales in the 1930s and the animals and farmers he encounters. Episodic, warm, funny, and told with the cadence of a storyteller who knows how to keep attention without dramatic structure. Herriot's voice is company in the most specific sense — you want to be in the room with him.

**Anne of Green Gables — L.M. Montgomery** · *Extended*
Anne Shirley is one of the great companions in fiction — imaginative, verbal, impossible, loveable. Adults returning to this book find it different from childhood: more aware of Marilla, of what the love costs, of what Montgomery is doing. An entry point for readers who want warmth without difficulty.

**The No. 1 Ladies' Detective Agency — Alexander McCall Smith** · *Extended*
Precious Ramotswe opens Botswana's only female-run detective agency. McCall Smith's Africa is warm and specific and gently comic. The book holds its readers with complete ease. For a reader who wants to be taken somewhere entirely different by a voice they trust completely.

**A Psalm for the Wild-Built — Becky Chambers** · *Extended*
A monk named Dex, restless and searching, meets a philosophically curious robot in a recovered wilderness. A novella about the question of what we need versus what we want. Short, warm, unhurried, and structured around precisely the question a restless reader might be carrying.

**The Guernsey Literary and Potato Peel Pie Society — Mary Ann Shaffer** · *Extended*
An epistolary novel about the literary society that formed on Guernsey during the German occupation. Warm, funny, about books and friendship and community under constraint. Giftable. One of those books that circulates among people who love books.

**Cannery Row — John Steinbeck** · *Extended*
Steinbeck's love letter to the disreputable community around a sardine cannery in Monterey. No plot. A series of portraits of people who live outside respectable society with more warmth and dignity than it usually credits them. Very short. Warm in the specific way of a community that has chosen each other.

**The Unlikely Pilgrimage of Harold Fry — Rachel Joyce** · *Extended*
A retired man decides to walk from Devon to Berwick-upon-Tweed to visit a dying friend, having no equipment and no plan. The walk becomes an internal journey. Joyce's warmth is genuine and the book is about the small distances between people who love each other.

**84 Charing Cross Road — Helene Hanff** · *Extended*
Twenty years of letters between a New York writer and a London bookshop. About books, about friendship conducted through books, about the specific warmth of long-distance correspondence with someone whose taste you trust. Very short. Perfect for the bibliophile who feels that reading is a relationship, not a transaction.

## 6. Perspective

*9 books — 9 Core, 0 Extended*

**Why this category:** All Core. These are books that shift how the reader sees — not through argument but through experience. They do not tell you what to think about mortality or gender or power or identity. They put you inside a perspective until you understand it from within.

**Who arrives here:** Someone who feels too close to their own situation. Someone who needs distance — not escape, but a different angle on the world. Someone ready to be changed by what they read.

---

**Never Let Me Go — Kazuo Ishiguro** · *Core*
Students at an English boarding school whose purpose is slowly, quietly revealed. Kathy's narration is gentle and slightly distant, and the accumulation of what she doesn't say is where the grief lives. About acceptance and complicity and what it means to live within a system without questioning it. Stays.

**Things Fall Apart — Chinua Achebe** · *Core*
Okonkwo is a respected man in the Igbo community of Umuofia — proud, strong, afraid of failure. Then the missionaries arrive. Achebe's novel is the counternarrative to every colonial adventure story. Brief, precise, and indispensable. Belongs in every serious library.

**The Awakening — Kate Chopin** · *Core*
A woman in 1890s New Orleans slowly, impossibly, becomes herself. Published in 1899 and almost buried by its reception. One of the shortest and most devastating novels in American literature — about what a woman might want and the price of wanting it. Core because the specific reader it serves (and shocks) is real and present now.

**The Death of Ivan Ilyich — Leo Tolstoy** · *Core*
A successful judge becomes ill and, as he dies, understands that he has spent his life doing the acceptable thing and missing the actual thing. Sixty-five pages. Tolstoy's most perfectly formed work. For the reader who needs permission to stop caring about what looks correct and start caring about what is true.

**Flowers for Algernon — Daniel Keyes** · *Core*
Charlie Gordon undergoes surgery that temporarily makes him brilliant. The novel is written as his progress reports, and the reader watches the prose change — then watches it change back. An irreversible reading experience. Once you've watched the writing shift, you cannot unknow what it means.

**The Stranger — Albert Camus** · *Core*
Meursault attends his mother's funeral, begins a relationship, commits a murder, and is tried — largely for the fact that he did not cry at the funeral. Short, strange, and unexpectedly funny in places. One of those books whose meaning shifts with every reading and every decade of life.

**Pedro Páramo — Juan Rulfo** · *Core*
A man travels to his father's village and finds it populated by the dead. One hundred pages of Mexican magical realism that reads like something that has always existed rather than something that was written. Dense, beautiful, and the book that García Márquez said he memorised. For the reader who wants their understanding of narrative to be permanently enlarged.

**Mrs Dalloway — Virginia Woolf** · *Core*
Clarissa Dalloway prepares for her party. Septimus Warren Smith walks through London's parks. One day. Woolf's stream-of-consciousness novel remains the most precise description of how time actually feels — not chronological but associative, layered, simultaneous. Essential.

**The House on Mango Street — Sandra Cisneros** · *Core*
Esperanza grows up in a Latino neighbourhood in Chicago, and tells it in vignettes — brief, lyrical, and precise. The book is about place and identity and what a girl sees that the adults around her have stopped seeing. Readable in an afternoon. Stays for considerably longer.

## 7. Returning to Reading

*9 books — 0 Core, 9 Extended*

**Why this category:** All Extended by design. These books serve a specific and important reader: someone who has been away from reading — through exhaustion, through life, through the sense that there is nothing to read that will hold them — and is coming back. These books do not make demands. They welcome.

**Who arrives here:** A reader who says "I used to read." A reader who has tried three books in the last year and abandoned all of them. A reader who loved reading once and has lost the thread.

---

**Eleanor Oliphant Is Completely Fine — Gail Honeyman** · *Extended*
Eleanor's literal, precise narration is funny before it is heartbreaking. The book carries its reader with a voice that does not allow abandonment. For the reader who needs to remember that a first-person voice can be one of the most specific forms of company available.

**Lessons in Chemistry — Bonnie Garmus** · *Extended*
A chemist in the 1960s becomes the host of a cooking show and insists on treating it as a chemistry lesson. Warm, funny, propulsive, and confident. Garmus's prose moves fast and carries the reader. For someone who wants forward momentum and warmth simultaneously.

**The Thursday Murder Club — Richard Osman** · *Extended*
Four sharp retirees solve murders in their spare time. Pure entertainment crafted with intelligence. The book does not pretend to be anything other than what it is, and that honesty is part of its appeal. For a reader who needs to be reminded that reading can be uncomplicated pleasure.

**Project Hail Mary — Andy Weir** · *Extended*
An astronaut wakes up alone on a spacecraft with no memory, far from Earth, and has to figure out why. Science-forward, problem-solving in structure, warm in voice. Each chapter closes a door and opens another. For the reader who needs momentum above all else and does not want to stop.

**The House in the Cerulean Sea — TJ Klune** · *Extended*
A meek caseworker is sent to inspect a peculiar orphanage on an island. Warm, gentle, and deliberately easy — a book about belonging that is also itself a place to belong. For the reader who needs to feel safe while reading.

**The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy — Douglas Adams** · *Extended*
Arthur Dent's house is demolished the same morning the Earth is demolished to make way for a hyperspace bypass. Adams's humor is dry, warm, and completely specific. For the reader who has forgotten that reading can produce genuine laughter.

**Miss Pettigrew Lives for a Day — Winifred Watson** · *Extended*
A prim English governess stumbles into a day in Bohemian London in the 1930s that changes everything. Published in 1938, largely forgotten, recently rediscovered. Brief, warm, comic, and about the specific liberation of deciding it is not too late to live differently. Giftable.

**Good Omens — Terry Pratchett & Neil Gaiman** · *Extended*
An angel and a demon who have grown fond of Earth try to prevent the Apocalypse. Funny, warm, theologically knowing, and propulsive. For the reader who responds to wit and needs a book to make them glad they started reading it.

**The Princess Bride — William Goldman** · *Extended*
A grandfather reads his grandson an old book about Westley, Buttercup, Inigo Montoya, and the revenge of the Dread Pirate Roberts. Goldman's framing device is itself a meditation on why we tell stories to each other. For the reader who wants pure story — plot and wit and heart — and has temporarily forgotten this is enough.

## 8. Swedish Voices

*10 books — 6 Core, 4 Extended*

**Why this category:** Not a token category — a foundational one. Bokhyllan is a Swedish literary bookshop. Its shelves must hold Swedish literature in the way a knowledgeable Swedish reader would expect them to. This means both the canonical and the contemporary, the widely read and the somewhat forgotten. Tove Jansson is here in her literary prose, not as the Moomin author. Selma Lagerlöf is here because she belongs in every Swedish library. Jonas Hassen Khemiri is here because he is the most important Swedish literary voice of his generation.

**Who arrives here:** A reader specifically seeking Swedish literature. Also: any reader who receives a recommendation from this category, regardless of language, because Bokhyllan offers books in Swedish or in translation where available.

---

**Sommarboken — Tove Jansson** · *Core*
An old woman and her granddaughter spend a summer on a small island in the Finnish archipelago. Episodes, observations, silences. The most Bokhyllan book in the entire proposal — warm, still, precise, short, re-readable, and quietly wise about age and childhood and what they understand about each other that the middle years cannot. Essential.

**Gösta Berlings saga — Selma Lagerlöf** · *Core*
A dismissed clergyman and a band of cavaliers take over an estate for a year. Lagerlöf's novel is mythological, passionate, and formally unlike anything around it when published in 1891. The first Swedish novel to win the Nobel author its author. Belongs on any Swedish shelf not as heritage but as reading.

**Doktor Glas — Hjalmar Söderberg** · *Core*
A Stockholm physician keeps a diary in which he considers whether to kill a man he despises. Written in 1905 in prose so clean and modern it reads as contemporary. About conscience, desire, loneliness, and what we tell ourselves to justify what we want. Short, precise, and one of the most underread Swedish novels internationally.

**Utvandrarna — Vilhelm Moberg** · *Core*
The Emigrants: Karl Oskar and Kristina leave Småland for America in the mid-nineteenth century. The opening novel in Moberg's tetralogy is complete in itself and is foundational Swedish literature — about poverty, endurance, the choice to leave, and what is lost in that choice. Too few non-Swedish readers know this book.

**Allt jag inte minns — Jonas Hassen Khemiri** · *Core*
A novel assembled from the accounts of people who knew Samuel, who is dead before the book begins. Khemiri's formal invention here is remarkable — the book builds Samuel from fragments, none of them complete, all of them partial. About memory, identity, Swedishness, and what we cannot know about the people we are closest to. The most important Swedish novel of the past decade.

**Livläkarens besök — Per Olov Enquist** · *Core*
The Court Physician's Visit: the story of the German physician Struensee, who becomes the lover of the Danish queen and briefly transforms Denmark. Enquist's prose is precise and cool and the novel is about what happens when an Enlightenment idealist meets real power. Significant Swedish literary fiction that deserves to be known beyond Scandinavia.

**Kallocain — Karin Boye** · *Extended*
A totalitarian state, a chemist, and a truth drug that reveals everything citizens are forbidden to think. Published in 1940. Boye's dystopia is more psychologically internal than Orwell's, more interested in what the state does to the capacity to feel. Important, short, and formally different from everything else in this library.

**Populärmusik från Vittula — Mikael Niemi** · *Extended*
Growing up in the very far north of Sweden, on the Finnish border, in the 1960s and 1970s. Niemi's novel is funny, specific, warm, and set in a corner of Sweden that Swedish literature rarely visits. About music, friendship, and the particular texture of a place that forms you.

**Ronja Rövardotter — Astrid Lindgren** · *Extended*
Placed in this library for adult readers returning to a book they loved as children, and for adults who never read it but should. Lindgren's prose is as good as she is given credit for — clear, strong, and genuinely wild. The story of Ronja and Birk and the bandit families who are their parents is about loyalty, freedom, and the courage to love across a divide.

**En man som heter Ove — Fredrik Backman** · *Extended*
A curmudgeonly widower who has stopped wanting to live is slowly pulled back into the world by his new neighbors. Backman is a commercial novelist in a way that some of the others here are not — but this book earns its warmth, and its grief for Ove's dead wife is real. The most giftable Swedish novel in the library.

## 9. International Voices

*9 books — 3 Core, 6 Extended*

**Why this category:** Books from outside the Anglophone literary tradition that Swedish readers encounter in translation. Not an "international" section in the marketing sense — a genuine expansion of what the library knows about. These books are here because their reading experience is distinct from anything the English-language sections can offer.

**Who arrives here:** Any reader — not a category-specific arrival point. These books are distributed throughout recommendations where the match is right, with the international origin noted where relevant to a Swedish reader.

---

**My Brilliant Friend — Elena Ferrante** · *Core*
Elena and Lila, two girls growing up in a poor Naples neighbourhood in the 1950s. Ferrante's voice is unlike anything in translated fiction — intimate, ferocious, and precise about the specific texture of a female friendship and what it demands. The first volume in the Neapolitan quartet, complete in itself, and the entry point for a series that can hold a reader for months.

**If This Is a Man — Primo Levi** · *Core*
Levi's account of a year in Auschwitz, written with the precision of a chemist and the humanity of someone who spent his life afterward trying to understand what he had witnessed. Not a grief book in the conventional sense — it is a document and a testimony and an act of witness. Essential to any library that takes human experience seriously.

**The Leopard — Giuseppe Tomasi di Lampedusa** · *Core*
An ageing Sicilian prince watches his world dissolve as Italian unification transforms everything around him. Di Lampedusa's only novel, written at the end of his life and rejected twice before publication. About time, decline, the beauty of what is passing, and the specific sadness of an aristocratic consciousness watching its own extinction. One of the great novels of the twentieth century.

**The Elegance of the Hedgehog — Muriel Barbery** · *Extended*
A Parisian concierge and a twelve-year-old girl discover each other. Barbery's novel is philosophical and warm and about the places intelligence and beauty appear where nobody is looking for them. Enormously popular in France and something of an acquired taste — but a genuine book for the reader it fits.

**Convenience Store Woman — Sayaka Murata** · *Extended*
Keiko has worked at the same convenience store for eighteen years and is happy there, in a way the people around her cannot comprehend. Very short, very precise, and quietly radical about what it means to live outside the expected forms. Murata's narrator is disorienting and specific and unlike any other voice in this library.

**Kokoro — Natsume Soseki** · *Extended*
A young student's friendship with an older man he calls Sensei — and the secret that Sensei carries. Written in 1914. Soseki's novel is quiet, precise, and about the specific loneliness of someone who has seen something about human nature that they cannot unsee. One of the central works of Japanese literature.

**Bonjour Tristesse — Françoise Sagan** · *Extended*
Eighteen-year-old Cécile, her widowed father, their summer on the Côte d'Azur, and what she does to prevent a change she cannot accept. Written when Sagan was eighteen. Very short, morally serious beneath its apparently hedonistic surface, and a novel that has aged better than its reputation suggests.

**The Reader — Bernhard Schlink** · *Extended*
A teenage boy and an older woman in 1950s Germany. Years later, as a law student, he sees her on trial for war crimes. About guilt, complicity, the inheritance of the Nazi generation, and what reading means when it is both the best thing about a person and insufficient to redeem them. Difficult and necessary.

**Kitchen — Banana Yoshimoto** · *Extended*
A young woman deals with the death of her grandmother, who was the last of her family, and finds herself connected to an unconventional household. Yoshimoto's prose is warm and quiet and specific about grief in a minor key — the grief of ordinary loss, small displacement, and the comfort of kitchens. Short, accessible, and a different kind of warmth from anything else in the library.

## 10. Essays & Reflection

*8 books — 5 Core, 3 Extended*

**Why this category:** Books that think aloud. Not self-help — no prescriptions, no programs. Prose that models a way of engaging with the world: attentive, honest, willing to stay with difficulty. These books are here because Bokhyllan is a literary bookshop, and a literary bookshop should know that thinking is a form of reading too.

**Who arrives here:** A reader who wants intellectual company. A reader who has a question they cannot name yet. A reader who thinks in prose rather than in arguments.

---

**Man's Search for Meaning — Viktor Frankl** · *Core*
Frankl's account of the concentration camps and the meaning-making he found possible within them. Small and does not waste your time. The meaning he arrives at is not cheap consolation — it is found under conditions of absolute loss, which is why it holds. One of the most important short books of the twentieth century.

**Factfulness — Hans Rosling** · *Core*
The world is better than you think, and here is the data. Rosling's Swedish voice is warm and personal; the book is accessible science communication at its best. Core partly because Rosling is Swedish and this is a Swedish library, and partly because the book genuinely does what it promises: it shifts how you read the news.

**A Room of One's Own — Virginia Woolf** · *Core*
A woman needs money and a room of her own if she is to write fiction. Woolf's extended essay develops this premise into one of the great acts of literary criticism and one of the great feminist arguments. Still necessary. Still completely alive.

**Letters to a Young Poet — Rainer Maria Rilke** · *Core*
Rilke's letters to a young soldier who sent him poems and asked for advice. Rilke's advice turns out not to be about poetry — it is about patience, solitude, difficulty, and the courage to live with questions that do not have answers. For any reader who is beginning something uncertain.

**Pilgrim at Tinker Creek — Annie Dillard** · *Core*
Dillard spent a year observing the natural world around a creek in Virginia and wrote about what she found with the rigor of a scientist and the attention of a mystic. The prose is extraordinary — dense, specific, joyful, occasionally terrifying. One of the great nature writing books and one of the great contemplative books simultaneously.

**The Comfort Book — Matt Haig** · *Extended*
Fragments — some two sentences, some several pages — from Haig's experience of depression and recovery. Designed to be dipped into rather than read linearly; meets a reader wherever their attention currently is. The most explicitly therapeutic book in the library, and the one most likely to be recommended to a reader in acute difficulty.

**Meditations — Marcus Aurelius** · *Extended*
The private notebook of a Roman emperor, never intended for publication. Two thousand years old and still useful because Marcus Aurelius was thinking about problems that have not changed: how to stay present, how to deal with what cannot be controlled, how to do what is required. Not a self-help book. A mind thinking.

**The Book of Disquiet — Fernando Pessoa** · *Extended*
A warehouse bookkeeper in Lisbon writes fragmentary observations on existence, his own inner life, and the passage of time. Pessoa never organised this material; it was assembled after his death. Can be read in any order. About the interior life of someone who does not express it to anyone — and the extraordinary richness of that life. For the reader who lives primarily inside their own mind.

## 11. Nature & Place

*9 books — 2 Core, 7 Extended*

**Why this category:** Books where a specific place — a mountain, a creek, a landscape, a season — is the book's real subject, and where the attention paid to that place becomes a form of attention paid to what it means to be alive in a body in a world. These are not travel books. They are books about being somewhere.

**Who arrives here:** A reader who is overstimulated by the abstract and needs to be grounded in the physical. A reader who has spent too long indoors, literally or figuratively.

---

**The Living Mountain — Nan Shepherd** · *Core*
Shepherd walked in the Cairngorm mountains of Scotland for decades and wrote about what she found there — not descriptions of peaks conquered, but the experience of being in a mountain, inside its weather and light and water. One of the great works of nature writing, not widely known, and precisely the kind of book Bokhyllan exists to discover.

**Braiding Sweetgrass — Robin Wall Kimmerer** · *Core*
A botanist who is also a member of the Citizen Potawatomi Nation writes about plants, indigenous knowledge, and what it might mean to be in reciprocal relationship with the natural world rather than merely extracting from it. Long, beautiful, and structurally unlike anything else in the library. Core because it carries a genuinely different philosophy.

**A Sand County Almanac — Aldo Leopold** · *Extended*
A year on a Wisconsin farm, month by month, and the ecological ethic that emerges from close observation. Leopold's prose is precise and quiet. The book argues for a land ethic — for extending the community of moral concern to include the soil, the water, the plants — without making it feel like an argument.

**Watership Down — Richard Adams** · *Extended*
Rabbits flee their warren and find a new home. One of those books whose literal subject (rabbits) is entirely the vehicle for its real subject (community, leadership, the relationship between freedom and safety, and what a place means to those who made it). Long, immersive, and more serious than its reputation as a children's book suggests.

**The Old Ways — Robert Macfarlane** · *Extended*
Macfarlane walks ancient paths in Britain and beyond and writes about what walking and place do to the mind. His prose is dense and specific and genuinely literary. For the reader who wants to be taken outdoors in language.

**Wild — Cheryl Strayed** · *Extended*
A woman hikes the Pacific Crest Trail alone, following the collapse of her marriage and her mother's death. A grief memoir structured around a physical journey. For the reader who needs to understand that movement can be a form of reckoning.

**The Shepherd's Life — James Rebanks** · *Extended*
A Lake District shepherd writes about sheep farming, the seasons, his family, and what it means to do work that has been done in the same landscape for a thousand years. About craft, rootedness, and the specific form of satisfaction available in work done with skill and care.

**Prodigal Summer — Barbara Kingsolver** · *Extended*
Three interconnected stories set in the southern Appalachians — a wildlife biologist, a young woman, an elderly farmer — told over one summer. Kingsolver's ecological knowledge is woven into the narrative without ever becoming didactic. The most novelistic book in this category.

**The Secret Garden — Frances Hodgson Burnett** · *Extended*
A girl discovers a locked garden and what growing things in it does to a grieving household. Placed here for adult readers, not children, because the book is genuinely about healing and attention and the specific power of a place that becomes yours. Re-readable and quietly therapeutic.

## 12. Science Fiction

*6 books — 5 Core, 1 Extended*

**Why this category:** Literary science fiction — work that uses the speculative as a way of seeing something about the present more clearly. Not genre entertainment. Not escapism. Science fiction that belongs on the shelf alongside the literary fiction in this library, serving the same reading states in a different register.

**Who arrives here:** A reader who is restless and curious and wants to be asked a question they haven't been asked before. A reader who thinks in hypotheticals. A reader who is ready for depth and wants it in an unfamiliar form.

---

**The Left Hand of Darkness — Ursula K. Le Guin** · *Core*
An envoy to a planet where inhabitants are ambisexual — and what the removal of gender assumption reveals about everything that depends on it. Le Guin's prose is literary and precise, and the book is genuinely philosophical in a way that does not slow it down. One of the foundational texts of serious science fiction.

**Solaris — Stanislaw Lem** · *Core*
Scientists study an ocean on a distant planet that may or may not be intelligent. The ocean begins responding to them. Lem's novel is about the limits of human understanding — about what happens when the thing you are studying cannot be comprehended in human terms. Dense, slow, and unlike anything else.

**Parable of the Sower — Octavia Butler** · *Core*
A teenager in a near-future California on the edge of collapse builds a religion out of the only truth she can find: that God is change and the only lasting power is adaptation. Butler's prose is plain and urgent and the book is one of the most uncomfortably prescient novels of the late twentieth century.

**The Handmaid's Tale — Margaret Atwood** · *Core*
Offred lives in Gilead, a theocratic future America where fertile women are enslaved as breeders. Atwood's book is not a distant dystopia — it is assembled entirely from historical precedents, which Atwood documented in the original notes. One of those novels whose relevance increases rather than dates.

**Kindred — Octavia Butler** · *Core*
A Black woman in 1976 Los Angeles begins involuntarily time-travelling to an antebellum Maryland plantation, where she must keep the white enslaver who will become her ancestor alive or she will cease to exist. Butler uses the time-travel device with complete precision — it is not decorative but structural. One of the most important American novels.

**Station Eleven — Emily St. John Mandel** · *Extended*
A flu pandemic collapses civilisation, and twenty years later a travelling Shakespearean theatre company moves through the Great Lakes region. Mandel's novel is about what survives and what it means that art survives. Accessible, beautifully structured, and the most optimistic book in this section.

## Coverage Audit

### Swedish Representation

**10 of 100 books are Swedish.** This is appropriate for a Swedish literary bookshop. The Swedish section spans from Lagerlöf (1891) to Khemiri (2012), includes both canonical literature and contemporary voices, and holds work ranging from the warmly accessible (Backman) to the formally demanding (Enquist).

**Gap:** No Swedish poetry. Harry Martinson's Aniara — a science-fiction poem — is missing from the Science Fiction section where it belongs. No Stig Dagerman, whose short career produced some of the most significant Swedish prose of the twentieth century. The Swedish section is strong but slightly conservative.

---

### Female Representation

**Approximately 42 books have a female author (including Jansson, Lagerlöf, Morrison, Le Guin, Butler, Atwood, Dillard, Woolf, Chopin, Ferrante, Strout, Patchett, Ivey, Macdonald, Shepherd, Kimmerer, Strayed, Honeyman, Garmus, Cisneros, Sagan, Murata, Yoshimoto, Montgomery, Joyce, Hanff, Burnett).**

This is close to parity. Not by design — by following the books that belong.

**Weak area:** Female voices in the Science Fiction section. Butler and Atwood appear, but Le Guin is the only woman in the literary SF tradition with a second title available (The Dispossessed is not here). Octavia Butler has two titles and neither feels redundant.

---

### International Representation

**Countries represented in the library:**
USA (approximately 35 titles), UK (15), Sweden (10), France (5), Italy (4), Japan (4), Germany (2), Russia (2), Mexico (2), Nigeria (1), Canada (2), New Zealand/Australia (1).

**Heavy:** American and British literature. This is partly unavoidable — the English-language literary tradition is what Bokhyllan's voice is closest to — but it should be addressed in the next 100 books.

**Missing:** No Arabic literature. No Chinese literature. No South Asian literature (Tagore, Narayan, Lahiri). No African literature outside Achebe. No Latin American literature outside García Márquez and Rulfo. The International Voices section is too European.

**Italy is over-represented:** Three Core Italian books (Ferrante, Levi, di Lampedusa). This is defensible — all three are genuinely essential — but it should be noted.

---

### Fiction / Non-Fiction Balance

**Approximately 84 fiction, 16 non-fiction.**

The non-fiction is clustered in Essays & Reflection and Nature & Place. This is probably the right ratio for Bokhyllan's identity. A literary bookshop that is half non-fiction would feel different.

**Gap:** No biography. No history. The library has no book that serves a reader who wants to understand a specific historical period or person. This could be addressed with one or two additions in the next tranche.

---

### Modern / Classic Balance

**Approximately 55 books published post-1950, 30 books published 1900–1950, 15 books published pre-1900.**

The pre-1900 titles are all Core: Tolstoy, Chopin, Lagerlöf, Söderberg, Austen is missing — should she be here? Mrs Bridge, The Awakening. The classics are selective rather than comprehensive, which is correct.

**Gap:** The library is slightly thin on early-to-mid twentieth century literature between the classics and the contemporary. No Kafka. No Beckett. No Thomas Mann. No Conrad. These may not all belong in Bokhyllan's register, but their absence is notable.

**Note on Kafka:** The Book of Disquiet (Pessoa) does some of the work Kafka might do, but differently. Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell does some of what Kafka does, differently again. The surrealist and absurdist tradition is present but not through Kafka himself. Whether Kafka belongs on Bokhyllan's shelves is a genuine editorial question.

---

### Genre Balance

The library currently has:
- Literary fiction: ~55 books
- Non-fiction/essays: ~16 books
- Science fiction: 6 books
- Fantasy: 4 books (The Hobbit, Jonathan Strange, The Little Prince, The Master and Margarita — the last two are fantasy in the broad sense)
- Crime/mystery: 1 book (Thursday Murder Club)
- Children's/YA: 3 books (Anne of Green Gables, Ronja Rövardotter, The Secret Garden) — all recommended for adult readers

**The library is deliberately literary.** This is correct for Bokhyllan's identity. The one thriller is there specifically for the "returning to reading" function. This balance should be maintained as the library grows: additional genre fiction should justify its place by serving a reading state that literary fiction does not serve, not simply by being popular.

---

### Missing Areas

The most significant gaps identified in the Enrichment Experiment evaluation persist:

1. **Swedish poetry** — at minimum one Tranströmer selection
2. **African literature beyond Achebe** — Ngugi wa Thiong'o, Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, Aminatta Forna
3. **South Asian literature** — a significant absence
4. **Biography** — nothing here serves the reader who wants to understand a specific life
5. **A book about music** — music is as central to human emotional experience as any of the states this library addresses
6. **A book about visual art** — similar argument
7. **A quiet Swedish contemporary female voice** — the Swedish section is dominated by male and historical authors

## The Shape of Bokhyllan

*If a reader browsed these 100 books without seeing the logo, what would they conclude about the bookstore that chose them?*

---

They would conclude that whoever chose these books reads slowly and returns to things.

The books share certain qualities that are not on any list. They are almost all books that can be reread — that mean different things at different ages, in different difficulties, at different distances from their subject. There are no thrillers for the sake of pace. There are no books that exist to be consumed and discarded. Everything here has been chosen because it does something specific that cannot easily be replaced.

They would notice that the bookshop is interested in quiet. Not silence, not passivity — but the kind of attention that stillness makes possible. Stoner. Gilead. The Living Mountain. Sommarboken. Doktor Glas. The book on these shelves that makes the most noise is The Master and Margarita, which is also, in its way, an act of careful attention — to what survives under a totalitarian regime, to what laughter can do that argument cannot.

They would notice that the bookshop cares about Swedish literature in a way that suggests it actually knows it. Not as heritage. Not as a required category. Khemiri's Allt jag inte minns and Söderberg's Doktor Glas on the same shelf — a century apart, both formally precise, both interested in what a mind does to itself in private. That is a curation, not a collection.

They would notice that the bookshop respects the reader. Nothing here is condescending. The easier books — A Gentleman in Moscow, The Hitchhiker's Guide, Ronja Rövardotter — are easier because that is their quality, not because they were added to make the shelf accessible. The harder books — Solaris, One Hundred Years of Solitude, The Book of Disquiet — are here because they belong, not because difficulty is a signal of seriousness.

They would notice that the bookshop knows something about grief. The grief section is the most uncompromising in the library — Didion, Morrison, Levi, Macdonald, Bauby, Ness. None of these books offer easy comfort. All of them are honest about what grief is, which is the only form of comfort that works on a reader who is actually grieving. A bookshop that has this section means what it says.

They would probably conclude that whoever chose these books is a reader who believes that reading is one of the things that makes a life navigable. Not the only thing. But one of them. And that this belief was formed not by reading about reading but by reading — deeply, over time, with the specific kind of attention that accumulates into taste.

That is the bookshop.

The taste is warm but not soft. Literary but not academic. Wide but not encyclopedic. Swedish but not provincial. Old but not nostalgic. New but not trendy.

The philosophy is that a good book placed at the right moment can change how someone moves through a difficult month. Not by solving anything. By being there.

The atmosphere is the atmosphere of a room where books have been chosen by someone who read them.

That is what Bokhyllan is trying to be.
