# Mood Taxonomy v1

## Purpose

This document defines Bokhyllan's controlled mood vocabulary.

Moods capture the reader-facing emotional feel or atmosphere of a book. They are separate from genre, theme, tags, curator notes, enrichment drafts, and final recommendation copy.

This is documentation only. It does not change runtime types, book data, UI, or recommendation logic.

## What Moods Are

Moods describe how a book tends to feel to read:

- the emotional atmosphere
- the felt temperature of the prose
- the reader-facing tone
- the kind of inner weather a book creates
- the affective quality a recommendation may need to match

Mood should answer: what does the reading experience feel like?

## What Moods Are Not

Moods are not:

- genres
- themes
- marketing labels
- plot summaries
- quality judgments
- final recommendation copy
- reader personas
- proof that a book is gentle, serious, or important

A mood should not explain why the book is good. It should name the felt register a curator would need to consider during matching.

## Mood Definitions

### `quiet`

Definition:

Low-volume, restrained, calm, and unshowy in its emotional presentation.

When to use:

Use when the book feels gentle in volume even if its subject is serious.

When not to use:

Do not use simply because a book is short or slow.

### `still`

Definition:

Paused, settled, patient, and attentive; a book that invites the reader to stop moving.

When to use:

Use when stillness is central to the reading experience.

When not to use:

Do not use for any book that is merely uneventful.

### `warm`

Definition:

Human, generous, companionable, and emotionally hospitable.

When to use:

Use when warmth is a dominant felt quality.

When not to use:

Do not use as a synonym for comforting; a book can be warm without soothing.

### `tender`

Definition:

Soft, careful, vulnerable, and emotionally close.

When to use:

Use when the book treats people, memory, or pain with gentleness.

When not to use:

Do not use for sentimentality or sweetness alone.

### `comforting`

Definition:

Reassuring, steadying, or emotionally restorative.

When to use:

Use when the book may help a reader feel held or less alone.

When not to use:

Do not use for any book that merely ends well.

### `reflective`

Definition:

Thoughtful, inward, and shaped by consideration or looking back.

When to use:

Use when reflection is a major part of the reading texture.

When not to use:

Do not use for any book with occasional introspection.

### `meditative`

Definition:

Contemplative, slow-breathing, and focused on presence, perception, or sustained thought.

When to use:

Use when the book asks the reader to dwell rather than move quickly.

When not to use:

Do not use as a generic label for slow pacing.

### `clear`

Definition:

Lucid, precise, clean-lined, and emotionally or intellectually clarifying.

When to use:

Use when the book feels like it sharpens perception.

When not to use:

Do not use simply because the prose is easy to understand.

### `luminous`

Definition:

Light-bearing, radiant, quietly bright, or spiritually/intellectually illuminated.

When to use:

Use when the book has a felt glow or sense of revealed attention.

When not to use:

Do not use as a prestige word for beautiful writing.

### `hopeful`

Definition:

Carrying possibility, repair, future-facing tenderness, or faith in continuance.

When to use:

Use when hope is part of the book's felt landing.

When not to use:

Do not use for optimism that feels easy or decorative.

### `bittersweet`

Definition:

Holding sweetness and sadness together without resolving either away.

When to use:

Use when the book leaves both ache and warmth.

When not to use:

Do not use for any book that is simply sad with a pleasant ending.

### `melancholic`

Definition:

Sorrowful, wistful, or shaded by loss, usually in a sustained and reflective way.

When to use:

Use when melancholy is a defining atmosphere.

When not to use:

Do not use for brief sadness or dramatic grief alone.

### `austere`

Definition:

Spare, severe, disciplined, unsentimental, and emotionally pared back.

When to use:

Use when restraint or severity is central to the reading feel.

When not to use:

Do not use merely because prose is concise.

### `dark`

Definition:

Heavy, shadowed, morally difficult, emotionally severe, or strongly troubled.

When to use:

Use when darkness is a major part of the reader-facing atmosphere.

When not to use:

Do not use for any book with sad events.

### `unsettling`

Definition:

Disturbing, destabilizing, uncanny, or emotionally difficult to settle after.

When to use:

Use when the book intentionally leaves the reader off balance.

When not to use:

Do not use for ordinary suspense or plot tension.

### `tense`

Definition:

Pressurized, suspenseful, tight, or emotionally taut.

When to use:

Use when tension shapes the reading experience.

When not to use:

Do not use for every book with conflict.

### `restless`

Definition:

Uneasy, searching, unsettled, or in motion without full release.

When to use:

Use when the book carries inner agitation or searching energy.

When not to use:

Do not use for fast pacing alone.

### `strange`

Definition:

Odd, uncanny, surprising, dreamlike, or quietly off-center.

When to use:

Use when strangeness is a felt texture.

When not to use:

Do not use for any unfamiliar setting or unusual premise.

### `playful`

Definition:

Light-footed, witty, formally curious, humorous, or delighted by movement and invention.

When to use:

Use when play is part of the book's felt appeal.

When not to use:

Do not use for all books with jokes or charm.

### `expansive`

Definition:

Wide, generous in scope, world-opening, or spacious in emotional/intellectual range.

When to use:

Use when the book makes the reader feel the world has widened.

When not to use:

Do not use merely because a book is long.

### `intimate`

Definition:

Close, private, interior, or emotionally near.

When to use:

Use when the book feels like a confidential or inward encounter.

When not to use:

Do not use for any first-person narration by default.

## Multi-Mood Rules

Recommended mood count:

- Prefer 1 primary mood.
- Add 1-2 secondary moods when they materially clarify reader fit.
- Use more than 3 only in a taxonomy review context.

Primary vs secondary moods:

- The first mood should describe the dominant reader-facing feel.
- Secondary moods should refine the experience, not list every possible reaction.

Avoid mood stuffing:

- Do not add moods for discoverability.
- Do not add every emotion the plot contains.
- Do not duplicate themes as moods.
- Do not use moods as praise.

Avoid subjective overreach:

- Mood is interpretive, but it should still be reviewable.
- Prefer a modest mood key over a dramatic one when unsure.
- If curators disagree strongly, choose the mood most useful for reader matching or leave the field for review.

## Relationship To Atlas v2

Mood may support future matching based on what the reader is seeking:

- a reader wanting something `comforting`
- a reader ready for something `dark`
- a reader seeking a `quiet` or `meditative` experience
- a reader drawn to something `strange`, `expansive`, or `intimate`

This taxonomy does not become the recommendation engine. It gives Atlas v2 a controlled vocabulary for reader-facing emotional feel so future matching can remain consistent and auditable.

## Future Compatibility

### Genres

Genres describe the broad kind of book. Mood describes the felt reading experience. A book can be `poetry` and feel `clear`, `luminous`, or `melancholic`.

### Themes

Themes describe what the book explores. Mood describes how it feels. A book about `grief` might be `tender`, `dark`, `comforting`, or `austere`.

### Tags

Tags may later describe flexible signals such as accessibility, form, texture, length, or use case. Mood should remain focused on emotional atmosphere.

### Curator Notes

Curator notes can explain why a mood matters for a particular reader. Mood keys should remain compact metadata, not prose.

### Enrichment Drafts

Future enrichment drafts can propose mood values for review. Approved moods should come from this list or be marked for taxonomy review.
