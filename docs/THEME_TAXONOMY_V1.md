# Theme Taxonomy v1

## Purpose

This document defines Bokhyllan's controlled theme vocabulary.

Themes describe what a book explores, helps with, or circles around. They are separate from genre, mood, tags, curator notes, and final recommendation copy.

This is documentation only. It does not add runtime fields, change book data, or implement recommendation logic.

## What Themes Are

Themes describe durable human subjects inside a book:

- what the book explores
- what the book helps a reader sit with
- what questions or life situations the book circles around
- what kind of inner or social material the book carries

Themes should be useful for future matching when a reader is seeking something like perspective, comfort, recognition, moral clarity, companionship, or room for grief.

## What Themes Are Not

Themes are not:

- genres
- moods
- marketing labels
- plot summaries
- recommendation copy
- reader personas
- retailer categories
- proof that a book is important

A theme should not explain the whole book. It should name a central concern clearly enough that another curator can understand why it was chosen.

## Theme Definitions

### `family`

Definition:

Relationships, obligations, inheritance, tension, tenderness, or harm within families.

When to use:

Use when family dynamics are central to the book's emotional or moral structure.

When not to use:

Do not use merely because a character has relatives.

### `friendship`

Definition:

Companionship, loyalty, chosen closeness, estrangement, or change between friends.

When to use:

Use when friendship meaningfully shapes the book.

When not to use:

Do not use for casual social contact.

### `love`

Definition:

Romantic love, enduring affection, attachment, desire, devotion, or love's consequences.

When to use:

Use when love is a central force or question.

When not to use:

Do not use for every book with a relationship subplot.

### `grief`

Definition:

The active experience of mourning, bereavement, absence, or living after death.

When to use:

Use when grief is central to the reader's encounter with the book.

When not to use:

Do not use for all sad books. Use `loss` when the theme is broader than mourning.

### `loss`

Definition:

Losing people, places, possibilities, identities, innocence, status, or time.

When to use:

Use when the book is shaped by what has disappeared or cannot be recovered.

When not to use:

Do not use as a generic synonym for sadness.

### `memory`

Definition:

Remembering, forgetting, reconstruction, testimony, nostalgia, or the instability of the past.

When to use:

Use when memory is a central mechanism or concern.

When not to use:

Do not use for any book that simply includes flashbacks.

### `aging`

Definition:

Growing older, late life, mortality, changing bodies, accumulated experience, or looking back.

When to use:

Use when age and the passage of life are central.

When not to use:

Do not use merely because an older character appears.

### `childhood`

Definition:

Childhood experience, innocence, dependence, early perception, or the lasting force of being young.

When to use:

Use when childhood is a major lens or subject.

When not to use:

Do not use for every book about a child if another theme is more central.

### `identity`

Definition:

Questions of selfhood, name, role, gender, culture, origin, or becoming.

When to use:

Use when the book explores who someone is or is allowed to become.

When not to use:

Do not use as a vague catch-all for character development.

### `belonging`

Definition:

The need to belong, exclusion, home, group membership, social fit, or being recognized.

When to use:

Use when belonging or not belonging shapes the book's stakes.

When not to use:

Do not use for any book with a setting or community.

### `loneliness`

Definition:

Isolation, emotional distance, social invisibility, or living without sufficient connection.

When to use:

Use when loneliness is central to the reader's emotional understanding.

When not to use:

Do not use for brief solitude or independence.

### `faith`

Definition:

Religious belief, spiritual trust, ritual, devotion, or metaphysical confidence.

When to use:

Use when faith is actively explored.

When not to use:

Do not use merely because religion appears in the setting.

### `doubt`

Definition:

Uncertainty, skepticism, spiritual struggle, moral hesitation, or questioning what once felt stable.

When to use:

Use when doubt is a central experience.

When not to use:

Do not use for ordinary suspense or plot uncertainty.

### `moral_choice`

Definition:

Ethical decisions, compromised action, conscience, guilt, or choosing between difficult goods.

When to use:

Use when the book turns on moral pressure or moral clarity.

When not to use:

Do not use for any book where a character simply makes decisions.

### `responsibility`

Definition:

Duty, care, obligation, accountability, promises, or what one owes others.

When to use:

Use when responsibility is a major burden or value.

When not to use:

Do not use for routine adult tasks.

### `freedom`

Definition:

Autonomy, escape, constraint, self-direction, captivity, or the cost of being free.

When to use:

Use when freedom or confinement shapes the book.

When not to use:

Do not use for every travel or adventure story.

### `work`

Definition:

Labor, vocation, craft, profession, service, status through work, or the meaning of doing a job.

When to use:

Use when work is central to identity, structure, or conflict.

When not to use:

Do not use merely because a character has a profession.

### `class`

Definition:

Social class, money, status, inequality, mobility, manners, or material constraint.

When to use:

Use when class shapes opportunity, relationships, or self-understanding.

When not to use:

Do not use for any mention of wealth or poverty.

### `place`

Definition:

Landscape, city, home, region, room, country, or the meaning of being somewhere.

When to use:

Use when place is central enough that the book would be different without it.

When not to use:

Do not use for every book with a vivid setting.

### `nature`

Definition:

The natural world, seasons, animals, land, sea, weather, ecology, or human relation to nature.

When to use:

Use when nature is a central presence or relation.

When not to use:

Do not use for decorative landscape description.

### `migration`

Definition:

Movement between countries, languages, homes, cultures, or conditions of exile and arrival.

When to use:

Use when migration shapes identity, family, belonging, or memory.

When not to use:

Do not use for ordinary travel.

### `war`

Definition:

War, occupation, aftermath, displacement, violence, moral pressure, or survival under conflict.

When to use:

Use when war or its aftermath is central.

When not to use:

Do not use for a passing historical reference.

### `healing`

Definition:

Recovery, repair, restoration, tenderness after harm, or learning to live after rupture.

When to use:

Use when the book offers or explores a process of repair.

When not to use:

Do not use simply because a book is comforting.

### `creativity`

Definition:

Art, making, imagination, writing, music, craft, or the cost and value of creation.

When to use:

Use when creative work or artistic attention is central.

When not to use:

Do not use for every book with beautiful prose.

### `attention`

Definition:

Seeing clearly, noticing, patience, perception, contemplation, or learning to attend to the world.

When to use:

Use when the book's value is strongly tied to attention or perception.

When not to use:

Do not use as a generic praise label.

### `solitude`

Definition:

Being alone as retreat, pressure, practice, refuge, or condition.

When to use:

Use when solitude is central and distinct from loneliness.

When not to use:

Do not use when the book is primarily about unwanted isolation; use `loneliness`.

### `community`

Definition:

Collective life, shared responsibility, local ties, social fabric, or belonging among people.

When to use:

Use when the group or community is a central force.

When not to use:

Do not use merely because a cast of characters exists.

### `power`

Definition:

Authority, control, hierarchy, coercion, institutional force, or domination.

When to use:

Use when power relations shape the book's central conflict or world.

When not to use:

Do not use for any disagreement or influence.

### `justice`

Definition:

Fairness, punishment, accountability, law, repair, injustice, or moral redress.

When to use:

Use when justice is a central question or pressure.

When not to use:

Do not use for every book involving wrongdoing.

### `time`

Definition:

Passing time, waiting, duration, lost time, historical time, aging time, or the pressure of temporality.

When to use:

Use when time itself is central to the book's structure or meaning.

When not to use:

Do not use merely because a story covers many years.

## Multi-Theme Rules

Recommended theme count:

- Prefer 2-4 themes per book.
- Use 1 theme only when a book is unusually focused.
- Use 5 only when every selected theme is central and useful.

Avoid theme stuffing:

- Do not list every topic that appears.
- Do not add themes for discoverability.
- Do not duplicate what genre, mood, or tags should express.
- Do not use themes to write a mini review.

Central vs incidental:

- A central theme shapes the book's reader experience.
- An incidental topic appears but could be removed without changing the book's core.
- Only central themes belong in this taxonomy.

## Relationship To Atlas v2

Themes may support future matching based on what the reader is seeking:

- a reader looking for recognition around grief
- a reader wanting perspective on responsibility
- a reader needing room for solitude
- a reader drawn to place, memory, or moral choice

This taxonomy does not implement that matching yet. It gives Atlas v2 a controlled vocabulary so future recommendation logic can reason about books without relying on free-form copy.

## Future Compatibility

### Genres

Genres answer what broad kind of book this is. Themes answer what the book explores. A book can be `literary_fiction` and explore `family`, `memory`, and `responsibility`.

### Mood

Mood describes the felt reading experience. Themes describe subject matter or human concern. A book about `grief` can feel `tender`, `dark`, `clear`, or `hopeful`.

### Tags

Tags may later capture flexible signals such as form, accessibility, texture, or use case. Themes should remain more stable and human-subject focused.

### Curator Notes

Curator notes can explain why a theme matters for a specific recommendation. Themes should remain compact keys, not prose.

### Enrichment Drafts

Future enrichment drafts can propose themes for review. Approved themes should be selected from this list or marked for taxonomy review.
